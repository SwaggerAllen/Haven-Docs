# Haven open questions

**Status: living ledger.** This document tracks the specific questions Haven’s design currently cannot answer on its own — questions that need expert eyes, practitioner experience, or research that doesn’t exist yet. It is published as part of the design-in-the-open methodology: the five open problems in the introductory series are the strategic statement of what’s unsolved; this ledger is the operational decomposition, maintained as questions are asked, answered, and closed.

How to read an entry: each question has an ID, a status (**open** / **asked**, with a link to the public thread where applicable / **answered**, with a link or summary / **closed**, with the resolution), and where one exists, a link to a review brief — a short standalone writeup of the construction, the claimed property, and what’s already been considered. Questions marked **(research)** are ones we believe could support standalone publishable work; if one of these is in your field and looks interesting, the design documents are the case study and we’d welcome the collaboration.

If you can answer any of these, or can point to prior art we’ve missed, we want to hear from you — publicly or privately. Answers given privately are summarized here only with permission, attributed or not as the answerer prefers.

## Franking and abusive-content reporting

Haven adopts asymmetric message franking (the Grubbs/Lu/Ristenpart 2017 through Namavari/Ristenpart 2024–25 line) for verifiable abuse reporting in E2EE contexts. The composition questions:

- **CR-F1 (research).** Composition of asymmetric franking with MLS PrivateMessages: commitment placement within the AEAD payload, choice of committing AEAD, and whether the composition reintroduces any invisible-salamanders-class behavior. *Status: open, sharpened by audit (2026-06-12).* Two refinements. (1) Prior art exists and is central: Namavari, Wang, Ristenpart et al., “Private Hierarchical Governance for Encrypted Messaging” (MlsGov), IEEE S&P 2024 — the first private-governance-over-MLS system, PolicyKit-inspired, with a working prototype. This is not a scoop but a foundation and comparison axis: Haven builds in the same quadrant but diverges on federation, communities-as-first-class-entities, schism, and (likely) threat model. The question becomes “we build on the MlsGov approach; here is where we diverge and the composition questions those divergences raise,” which is a stronger and warmer ask to the same researchers already identified as recipients. (2) Layering: MLS PrivateMessage already applies its own AEAD with application-chosen AAD (RFC 9420), so the construction must frank *underneath* (commit to plaintext, pass the opening through, let MLS encrypt the franked package) rather than trying to make MLS’s own AEAD committing. Implementation is straightforward given that layering plus salamander-avoidance; both are confirm-items for the reviewer, not novel risks. Open question to resolve on reading the paper: does MlsGov assume a semi-honest platform where Haven assumes a blind/untrusted operator? If so, that gap is both a Haven contribution and a real composition question.
- **CR-F2.** Franking over a sender-key broadcast layer riding on MLS-distributed keys — is there a cleaner construction than per-broadcast commitments? *Status: open, confirmed novel by audit (2026-06-12).* No published franking-over-sender-keys/Megolm construction found; the franking literature is pairwise or sealed-sender, all per-message. The core tension is real: franking needs a per-message commitment opening, while sender-key/Megolm efficiency comes from amortizing one ratcheted key over many messages — the two pull against each other, and nothing reconciles them for the broadcast case. Matrix’s own broadcast-layer work (e.g. MSC2399) is still at key-withholding notification, far from franking. Compounded for Haven by the MLS/sender-key integration. Likely the most novel item in the franking bundle and a clear reviewer question.
- **CR-F3.** Key-reveal escalation granularity for broken-franking recovery: per-message reveal vs. epoch-key reveal vs. exporter-derived moderation keys, and the interaction of each with MLS forward secrecy. *Status: open, sharpened by audit (2026-06-12).* It is a tradeoff against FS tolerance, but the literature has moved past pure trading toward FS-engineered constructions: Hecate (Issa et al., USENIX Security 2022) uses moderator-preprocessed tokens “intelligible only to its future self” and explicitly targets backward/post-compromise security; the key-evolving message franking line formalizes forward security with key-update algorithms. The sharpened question: do Hecate-style preprocessed tokens and/or key-evolving franking compose with Haven’s exporter-derived moderation keys to allow escalation without a per-message-reveal FS hit? Points at named constructions rather than an open-ended dial.
- **CR-F4.** Multi-layer verifier-key delegation (the same report verified at community, alliance, instance, and federation layers) and ephemeral jury re-verification without long-term key holding — are there known patterns? *Status: closed as a reviewer question (2026-06-12), splits into two design notes.* Delegation half cuts: the franking commitment sits outside the encryption and verification uses a verification key disjoint from any decryption key, so “more layers verify” is just more parties holding/handed the verification key — no encryption secret moves, and delivery is provable via the CT logs. Not novel; and if Haven ever needs per-layer commitments rather than one-commitment-many-verifiers, the construction is published: “Third-Party Moderation… with Multiple Moderators” (ePrint 2026/010, Jan 2026), whose `IssueCert(skM, id, texp)` issues moderator verification capability with built-in expiry. Jury-ephemerality half folds into Haven’s existing expiry-as-revocation discipline: time-bounded verification capability that genuinely expires is the `texp` / time-based-signature pattern (2026/010; Hecate’s Stamp step prevents indefinite token use post-compromise; the legal-theory redesign paper 2506.07041 names the problem and points at time-based signatures). So jury verification = an expiry-scoped capability like freshness credentials and per-epoch keys, not a novel construction. Net: no reviewer question; a design note that jury verification keys are issued per-term with expiry, citing 2026/010. External third parties (law enforcement) needing to verify without CT-log access is the portability concern tracked under CR-F5, and needs no decryption keys.
- **CR-F5.** Does transcript franking add value when the transport already provides authenticated per-mailbox total ordering? *Status: open, value-add confirmed by audit (2026-06-12); earlier “over-building” prior was wrong.* Transcript franking is a named primitive (Namavari & Ristenpart, “Transcript Franking for Encrypted Messaging,” arXiv 2507.19391, 2025, with proven constructions for two-party and group messaging) providing integrity over content *plus* cryptographic assurance of ordering and contiguity. It defends against an attack the CT log does not catch: a malicious reporter who undetectably *omits* messages from a reported subset, changing the meaning of what remains (the paper’s motivating example). Authenticated transport order and the verifiable log prove what happened in the mailbox; they do not prevent a reporter cherry-picking a non-contiguous subset to hand a moderator. The paper explicitly addresses the “but timestamps/transport already order things” objection and finds it insufficient for the omission property. Sharpened question for the bundle: Haven’s single-sequencer transport *does* provide authoritative ordering the general (asynchronous) setting lacks — does that simplify the transcript-franking construction or reduce what it must carry, and does the CT log close part of the contiguity gap or none of it? Strongest framing: “we read your 2025 transcript-franking paper; here is how it composes with our authenticated single-writer transport and CT logs.”
- **CR-F6.** State of the art on the all-malicious-group reporting gap — anything beyond governance/infrastructure-layer handling since the 2019–2025 papers? *Status: closed by audit (2026-06-12); prior confirmed, converts to a stance-with-citation rather than a question.* The gap is inherent, not open: message franking requires at least one honest participant to produce the opening (per CDT, “no third party gets content without approval of at least one participant”), so an all-malicious group defeats the entire franking class by construction. The only approaches that reach the all-malicious case are content-scanning ones — perceptual hashing and client-side scanning — which the SoK literature and civil society treat as the privacy-corrosive options Haven rejects (Scheffler et al., “SoK: Content Moderation for E2EE,” PETS 2023; “SoK: Content Moderation Schemes in E2EE Systems,” arXiv 2208.11147, updated through 2025). So for Haven the gap is genuinely unclosable at the crypto layer, and governance/infrastructure-layer handling is the correct and only answer — exactly what the study notes already said. No question to post. Citation gift: the architecture/study docs can now *state the position with authority* (“the all-malicious-group case is unclosable without client-side scanning, which Haven rejects; handled at the governance/infrastructure layer [SoK cites]”) rather than carry it as open. Also reinforces that the franking-bundle audience (Cornell Tech / Ristenpart / Grimmelmann orbit) is the right one — fourth appearance of that group as the authority in Haven’s space.

## Anonymous credentials (BBS)

Haven’s identity layer is a single BBS credential blind-issued over a committed secret `pid`, consumed by per-verifier pseudonyms, voting/ticketing nullifiers, and credential linking.

- **CR-B1 (research).** Does a single BBS credential, blind-issued over a committed secret `pid` plus disclosable attributes, securely support *simultaneously*: (a) selective disclosure, (b) per-verifier pseudonyms unlinkable across scopes, (c) nullifier soundness within scopes — with the issuer never learning `pid`? Plus device authorization as a bound value inside the proof. *Status: open, flagship, reshaped by audit (2026-06-12).* Fraunhofer 2025/824 is the right reference and a near-match, but its scope is “privacy-preserving revocation and device binding” — it does selective disclosure, non-duplication, revocation, device binding, but does not foreground the per-verifier-pseudonym / scoped-nullifier axis (b)+(c) that is load-bearing for Haven. So the missing requirement is exactly the pseudonym/nullifier conjunction; the construction remains novel. However, newer 2025 literature assembles more of the conjunction than the study notes assumed, across a different reference set: the Mayrhofer–Lehmann–shelat “Brief Note on Cryptographic Pseudonyms for Anonymous Credentials” (Oct 2025) does BBS pseudonyms with rate-limiting via Dodis–Yampolskiy PRF + range proof — the (b)+(c) pairing; “Device-Bound Anonymous Credentials With(out) Trusted Hardware” (ePrint 2025/1995) is a cleaner BBS device-binding reference and warns that a device public key is *not* a pseudonym (a conflation to check Haven’s design avoids); and the SoK “Anonymous Credentials for Digital Identity Wallets” (ePrint 2026/330) claims pseudonyms, deniability, partially-blind issuance, conditional disclosure, and issuer-hiding are “easily composable through Schnorr-type proof statements.” Reshaped brief question: does that Schnorr-composability claim cover Haven’s exact conjunction including blind-issued `pid`, and where is the single seam not already covered by the literature? Reading list for the brief: Fraunhofer 824, Mayrhofer pseudonyms note, device-binding 1995, SoK 2026/330, per-verifier-linkability draft -02.
- **CR-B2.** Credential-linking integration surface: does the CFRG BBS proof API as drafted expose cross-credential shared-attribute equality directly, or does it require a hand-composed Sigma conjunction over a shared blinded scalar? *Status: largely answered by spec reading (2026-06-12).* The core and blind drafts’ ProofGen operate on a single credential with no cross-credential interface. However, the per-verifier-linkability draft computes the pseudonym from the signed `pid` and the verifier context with proven correctness — so within a single verifier context (and a single ciphersuite/Interface, which the draft notes is required), presenting two credentials’ proofs and comparing pseudonyms links them by equality through the specified API alone, a pattern the draft itself suggests. Residuals: linking outside a pseudonym scope still requires composing below the API, and the soundness of cross-issuer pseudonym-equality linking is queued for review under CR-B1. A narrowed roadmap question (is in-API cross-credential equality future-extension territory?) may still go to CFRG.
- **CR-B3 (research).** Unlinkable attendance-count proof: prove “attended at least N attested in-person events within a trailing window” across communities, without cross-community linkability, composed from shared-`pid` linking and scoped nullifiers. This anchors Foundation-level governance counting; it is a new construction question, not just a composition check. *Status: open; brief in preparation.*
- **CR-B4.** Recount-publication linkability: if a per-scope counting authority publishes the full pseudonym→count multiset so any member can recount a tally, does publication create correlation surface across scopes? *Status: closed by analysis (2026-06-12), two halves.* Cryptographic half: the standard unlinkability definition for anonymous credentials already grants the adversary every presentation (collusion of all verifiers plus issuer), so bulk publication adds nothing the security model didn’t assume — if unlinkability holds, it holds against publication by definition (residual: confirm the per-verifier-linkability draft states its property in coalition form; folded into CR-B1 review). Statistical half: participation patterns are real correlation surface and no cryptography addresses them — the intersection/statistical-disclosure literature says small anonymity sets fall quickly. Design resolution: recount worksheets publish to members by default; per-vote disclosure scope is governance configuration, fixed before ballots are cast and never retroactively widened; the pattern leak is priced in the cumulative metadata-residuals accounting. Note the comparison point: members-only scope-local pseudonym worksheets are strictly more private than existing democratic practice (public voter participation files; public verifiable-election bulletin boards). Contested-vote escalation imports the accredited-observer / risk-limiting-audit pattern from election practice: auditor access pre-committed in the vote’s configuration, dormant until invoked.
- **CR-B5.** Blind-issuance soundness in Haven’s issuer topology: confirm a participating issuer (instance alliance for humanness; home community for membership credentials) cannot derive or forge an existing member’s pseudonym. *Status: closed as a reviewer question (2026-06-12), converted to a design assertion.* The BBS pseudonym construction makes `nym_secret` a two-part sum (`prover_nym + signer_nym_entropy`) where the prover’s half reaches the issuer only inside a blinding, hiding commitment, so the issuer cannot derive the pseudonym — non-reversibility of the commitment settles it, no review needed. Caveat that keeps this from being trivial: the same spec family also defines a “pseudonym with `pid` known to issuer” mode where the issuer generates `pid` and *can* derive the pseudonym. The non-derivability property therefore holds only under the prover-committed-`pid` mode. Resolution: Haven asserts prover-committed `pid` (two-part `nym_secret`, prover-only half) and explicitly does not use the issuer-known-`pid` mode; this assertion folds into the CR-B1 brief as a stated assumption, where naming the unused mode strengthens the composition question rather than adding a separate one.
- **CR-B6.** Issuer-hiding via the BBS-native randomizable-key line (ePrint 2026/369, /555, /870): public verifiability and composition with per-verifier pseudonyms and nullifiers. *Status: open; deliberately deferred (Phase 6) — tracked, not urgent.*
- **CR-M7.** Threshold issuance vs. short-lived freshness credentials: if freshness reissue is a genuine threshold operation, human coordination sits on the hot path; if delegated to an automated signer, the threshold property is decorative for the high-frequency path. *Status: closed as a threshold/FROST question (2026-06-12) — resolved by removing the threshold, not optimizing it.* Design decision: humanness issuance moves to single-signer, because the no-unilateral-minting property the threshold was protecting is now carried by three other layers built during the sybil work — rate-transparency logging (anomalous issuance is visible), attendance-anchoring (minted credentials carry no Foundation weight without attested attendance), and acceptance policy (every relying entity chooses which issuers to honor and can revoke acceptance / decertify). A single logged signing key is also *more* accountable than a k-of-n group for the decertification lever (one responsible party, one key to revoke). FROST / non-interactive threshold signing is therefore considered-but-not-adopted for humanness, not a review item. The freshness-reissue hot-path tension dissolves with the threshold. Residual to verify (becomes its own check, below): audit every security-critical consumer of humanness for a second detective/economic gate; single-signer is sound only where no consumer trusts raw humanness ungated.

## MLS composition and group-messaging internals

- **CR-M1.** In `draft-mcmillion` partition derivation, is epoch-in-Context redundant with exporter epoch-scoping, or deliberate explicit binding? *Status: answered by spec reading (2026-06-12), with a correction to the question itself.* The draft (draft-mcmillion-mimi-delivery-service, expired at -00) doesn’t specify the derivation at all — the exporter Label/Context choice is delegated by RFC 9420 to the deploying protocol, so the epoch-in-Context construction under question was our own. And it is redundant: RFC 9420’s key schedule folds the GroupContext (group id, epoch, tree hash, confirmed transcript hash) into the epoch secret from which the exporter secret descends, so every exporter output already binds the full group state — including distinguishing forked branches at the same epoch number, which is the property the partition-key design relies on. Resolution: Haven’s derivation drops the epoch from the Context and uses a distinct hardcoded label per RFC 9420 guidance. Residual: a courtesy note to the draft author that a revived draft should pin Label/Context for interop.
- **CR-M2.** Fork detection and recovery in a single-sequencer deployment: epoch-authenticator divergence attested to witness-cosigned transparency logs as fork proof, and the minimum incident-scoped state retention that preserves the most forward secrecy during reconciliation. *Status: substantially reframed by prior-art reading (2026-06-12).* Detection via epoch-authenticator comparison is canonical (RFC 9750 names it). The retention-vs-forward-secrecy tension is addressed in the literature: Alwen–Mularczyk–Tselekounis “Fork-Resilient Continuous Group Key Agreement” (ePrint 2023/394) introduces key puncturing so retained state can process fork branches without exposing child epochs, and Phoenix R&D’s DMLS (draft-kohbrok-mls-dmls, with a proof-of-concept on OpenMLS) specifies and implements it. What remains ours to design and verify: using DMLS as incident-scoped insurance in a single-sequencer deployment where forks are attack signatures rather than routine; the witness-log equivocation-proof pipeline; the reconciliation procedure; and composition questions (puncturing vs. our exporter-derived values; interaction with sub-group branching). Next step: read AMT and the DMLS draft in full, then a review brief in that frame. *(research)* flag retained for the composition questions. Two additions from the FED2 audit (2026-06-12): (1) **fork-safe replica promotion** is part of this question — if operator-disappearance failover promotes an allied replica to authoritative, the trigger must not allow a not-actually-dead operator and a promoted replica to both believe they are authoritative (a manufactured fork); who attests “operator gone” and how false-positive promotion is bounded is the same cryptographic problem as fork recovery, viewed from the other side. (2) The brief should preemptively address the obvious comparison — *why not Matrix State Resolution v2* — with: Matrix expects and merges forks as routine (its DAG has no authoritative writer), whereas Haven forbids forks via single-writer and treats any fork as exceptional attack-recovery; State Resolution v2 solves graceful merge, which is the problem Haven’s architecture chose to avoid rather than solve. *Update (2026-06-16): reading complete; review brief written and published alongside this ledger (see the CR-M2 fork-recovery brief), now out for external review.* The brief supersedes the retention framing above in one important way: the cost is not “incident-scoped” but a standing, rolling one. Because a fork is not detectable until after the fact, a member must speculatively retain each epoch’s recovery material until the group provably converges past it — and puncturing forward-secures only the init secret, so the load-bearing cost is the ratchet-tree private keys, retained in the clear. The open question is whether that cost can be reduced (forward-secure the keys short of HIBE; shorten the detection-latency-bounded window; or retain fewer keys despite blind retention). Replica-promotion attestation (the FED2 fold-in) is being handled as a design decision rather than a cryptographer question, and is not part of the brief’s asks.
- **CR-M3.** Per-epoch anonymous write-capability keypair construction and rotation (exporter-derived), composed with the blind delivery hub. *Status: closed as an open question — this is committed architecture, not an open research item (2026-06-12).* The architecture doc’s role-write enforcement model (the “three feed shapes,” §“Confidential sub-groups and the role-write enforcement model”) already specifies it: writers in role-write feeds are a branched sub-group with its own exporter; the write-cap public key is derived from that sub-group’s exporter with label `haven.write-cap.v1`, rotating per epoch; the hub verifies an anonymous outer write-cap signature (learns “an authorized writer acted,” not which one) while an inner persona signature inside the ciphertext provides reader-visible attribution and franking. This resolves the shared-secret-vs-distinguish-writers fork by the two-signature split: shared key for hub-facing anonymity (anonymity among writers is intended), inner signature for accountability. The remaining detail — exact keypair derivation from the exporter and rotation mechanics — is already tracked as an active investigation in the architecture doc, not a cryptographer question. The doc also already states the inherent caveat (a writer can leak the shared write-cap; mitigated by per-epoch rotation and inner-signature attributability). No fold-in to CR-B1; this is self-contained MLS/exporter construction.
- **CR-M4 (research).** Institutional archive-key construction: threshold decryption vs. proxy re-encryption for a deliberately-FS-broken archive, and rotating the archivist set without re-encrypting the whole archive. *Status: open but reshaped by audit (2026-06-12) — feasibility half solved, design half sharpened.* The “rotate holders without re-encrypting” half is a well-solved problem: Dynamic-committee Proactive Secret Sharing (DPSS) refreshes shares and alternates shareholders without changing the secret — named protocols CHURP (CCS 2019), COBRA (S&P 2022), MPSS, and long-term-secure storage systems built on it (PROPYLA) target exactly this archival use case. So the archive key stays fixed and only shares redistribute to a new archivist committee; cite rather than invent. The genuinely open half is untouched by DPSS: for the *deliberately FS-broken* archive, what is the right access architecture — threshold-decrypt the archive key on each audited access vs. proxy-re-encrypt to the requesting archivist — given their differing audit, exposure, and performance properties? New sharp edge the audit exposed: every archive access must be logged to the verifiable log (architecture doc), but DPSS *resharing* is itself a committee operation touching shares — does a reshare count as a loggable access, and how does committee handoff compose with the mandatory audit trail (the handoff being exactly the compromise event the trail exists to catch)? Fitness note: most PSS schemes assume secure erasure of old shares, which Haven cannot assume of archivists’ devices — ePrint 2026/1072 (PSS without erasures) is the relevant variant. Reading list: CHURP, COBRA, PROPYLA, 2026/1072.
- **CR-M5.** BBS device authorization composing with MLS leaf signature keys: what is the construction for a device-authorizing key bound to a pseudonym that is itself a group element rather than a signing key? *Status: open but downgraded from research to engineering integration (2026-06-12).* The composition (BBS pseudonym as MLS credential) is novel to Haven, but MLS provides a documented hook: RFC 9420 abstracts credential validation behind the Authentication Service and makes the application responsible for acceptable identifiers (TLS reference-identifier model), caring only that the AS vouches the credential is associated with the leaf’s `signature_key`. `draft-barnes-mls-addl-creds` defines a generic credential-binding structure and a `multi` credential type for exactly this (a `credential_key` corresponding to the credential as `signature_key` would), and `mls-rs` ships user-defined credentials with custom validation routines that “bridge to existing credential schemes.” So this is integration against a named pattern, not a research question. One residual, which rides into the CR-B1 brief rather than the MLS list: MLS leaf signing keys rotate on Update for forward secrecy, while a per-group pseudonym must be a stable scoped identity — the construction must bind the pseudonym to a rotating leaf key without making the pseudonym a signing key and without key rotation unbinding the scoped identity (cf. the “dpk is not a pseudonym” caution in device-binding ePrint 2025/1995).
- **CR-M6.** MLS at ~10,000 members (the Foundation Alliance group): real-world commit and tree-operation performance at that scale, and the fitness of current library options for external-credential validation hooks. *Status: open.*

## Federation and distributed systems

- **FED1.** Haven runs a blind delivery hub — hub-and-followers topology without hub state-tracking, a knowing divergence from the MIMI mainline, with the in-person join model removing the need for hub GroupInfo reconstruction. What are we underestimating about the maintenance and interop cost? *Status: open, sharpened sharply by audit (2026-06-12).* The divergence is real and well-defined, not Haven’s invention: MIMI had two competing delivery-service drafts. **draft-robert-mimi-delivery-service** (Robert & Kohbrok) is the state-tracking design — the hub tracks group state by storing proposals and processing commits, serves GroupInfo to joiners, and requires handshake messages as PublicMessages so the hub can follow along. **draft-mcmillion-mimi-delivery-service** (titled “Robust and Privacy-Preserving Delivery Service”) is the partition-key design Haven adopted, where the hub stays blind. The fork is the metadata-vs-functionality tradeoff in its purest form: state-tracking buys hub-served GroupInfo at the cost of the hub seeing handshake structure (a metadata leak Haven’s threat model refuses); the blind/partition-key approach preserves that privacy but loses hub GroupInfo reconstruction — which is exactly why Haven requires in-person/member-assisted joins. The WG mainline appears to have favored the state-tracking model (robert at -06 maturity vs mcmillion at -00). So FED1 becomes a precise, evidence-backed history question rather than a soft self-assessment: “the WG favored the state-tracking DS over the privacy-preserving partition-key design — what drove that, and what capabilities did the partition-key approach give up that the WG considered essential?” The answer is the catalog of what Haven gives up by going blind = exactly what FED1 asks. **Warm-door bonus:** Kohbrok co-authors the state-tracking draft *and* is the DMLS/Phoenix R&D contact already on the CR-M2 list — the person best positioned to explain why MIMI favored state-tracking is the same warm contact, and FED1 + CR-M2 are one coherent conversation with him. Reading task before posting: confirm which draft the MIMI WG actually adopted as its WG document and current state, to cite the fork accurately (same precision discipline as CR-M1).

**Layered-defense thesis (answer to “inconvenient, not impossible is no protection at all” — for the metadata-residuals writeup, critique item 3):** Haven does not claim correlation-resistance; it claims that correlation, even when successful, buys an attacker little and costs them a lot. The correlation attack is only the first of several barriers, and the cryptography’s job is to make the attack not-worth-it, not impossible: (1) technical friction (partition keys + anonymous BBS) converts impulsive operator action into deliberate sustained effort; (2) social friction — the operator is overriding a *visible democratic decision*, often against people they know personally (not a cryptographic barrier, so the critique can’t touch it); (3) exit cost — schism and portability mean a targeted community leaves intact, so the attack doesn’t even achieve its goal, it just costs the operator standing and members; (4) scale economics — continuously re-correlating every community on a large server across rotating partition keys is expensive per-community and continuous, so it doesn’t scale to mass surveillance. This is the opposite of privacy theater: theater is appearance substituting for protection, whereas here the protection is real but located in the *consequences and economics* of the attack rather than its cryptographic impossibility, and the honest disclosure of property-2 leakage is precisely what keeps users from being misled. Haven-specific amplifier: because governance decisions are committed to MLS state, the encrypted state is the governance record, so an operator defeating it doesn’t merely read messages — it visibly attacks the integrity of the democratic substrate, exposed by transparency logs and representative reconciliation. The stakes of breaking governance-bearing encrypted state are far above the generic-messaging case, which makes Haven’s acceptance of the correlation residual more defensible in context than the same residual would be in a general messenger (a property MIMI’s DS, not carrying governance, wouldn’t weigh).

- **FED2.** Single-authoritative-writer liveness: failover patterns for operator *disappearance* (not hostility) — detection thresholds, degraded read-only service from allied replicas during an authority gap, and what’s automatable vs. what requires representative ceremony. *Status: dissolved as a standalone question by audit (2026-06-12) — splits into a design decision and a fold into CR-M2.* The “what failover pattern” framing rests on a tradeoff Haven has already resolved: Matrix-style availability-under-partition is a property of its DAG (rooms belong to no server; State Resolution v2 merges forks on partition-heal), which is the structural opposite of single-authoritative-writer and cannot be duplicated without giving up the no-split-brain guarantee. Haven took the other end of that tradeoff deliberately. Design decision (Haven’s, not a reviewer question): **pre-committed conditional failover** — a community with an allied replica configures in advance, behind multisig, “under circumstances X, cut over to replica Y,” so cutover fires without a fresh approval round; instances without a replica keep the manual multisig migration path; communities choose whether and how long to wait for a missing operator. The genuinely open part is a *correctness* question, not an ops one, and it folds into CR-M2: who attests “operator gone,” how false-positive promotion is bounded, and how cutover composes with fork-detection so a premature trigger doesn’t itself cause the split-brain the single-writer model exists to prevent. The remaining fediverse-ops residual (can an allied replica serve degraded read traffic during the gap) is answerable from homeserver-admin experience — and the Matrix.org Sept-2025 outage (55TB restore, extended downtime because a *user’s* homeserver, unlike a room, is not replicated) confirms operator-disappearance is real and underserved even in the DAG world, making Haven’s replica+backup design a strength to describe rather than a gap to ask about.
- **FED3.** The replica-server pattern (public-facing replica URL, member-facing home URL) is analogous to AT Protocol’s PDS/relay split. What has separating authoritative hosting from content distribution cost in practice, and how should thundering-herd events against small origins be handled? *Status: open, transformed by audit (2026-06-12) into a strong position plus one genuine question.* Three findings. (1) **No firehose — the disanalogy holds and matters.** atproto’s re-centralization is specifically a *relay* phenomenon: the relay aggregates all PDSes into a global firehose, running an independent full-network relay means duplicating a multimillion-dollar operation, so everyone defaults to Bluesky’s — “whoever controls the relay controls what gets seen, hidden, or deprioritized.” Haven has no equivalent: discovery is browse-by-public-web-request (architecture doc confirms no aggregation, no federation required to browse), so the relay tax structurally doesn’t transfer. (2) **Read-only external surface means distribution concentration ≠ power concentration.** Haven’s public content is read-only to outsiders, so virality scales only read load (a CDN/caching problem) not write load, and a large replica/registry serving reads can’t deprioritize or rank — it doesn’t aggregate. This dissolves the “thundering-herd” sub-question: replicas are read-caches, the origin’s write path never sees the herd. (3) **The lived-gravity warning, which is the genuinely valuable part.** The atproto critique is sociological, not architectural: “at every layer, anyone can run their own; at every layer, almost nobody does” (the Gmail/email analogy — openness didn’t prevent centralization). Haven’s decentralization story rests on the same “anyone can self-host” claim, so the real open question is **what force makes communities exercise the self-host choice rather than defaulting to a big shared registry.** Haven has two counter-gravity mechanisms atproto lacks: the **in-person gate on registry-joining** (a registry can’t frictionlessly accrete the network; membership is rate-limited by physical contact) and the read-only surface (concentration ≠ control). The sharp question for the atproto/AT-WG community: “is an in-person membership gate actually enough to resist the default-to-the-big-provider gravity you watched happen, or does that gravity find a way regardless?” — a question their veterans would value because it applies their scar tissue to a design that took their lesson seriously. Credibility-positive Wave 1/2 question. *Related deferred design gap (logged, not urgent): a community that finds a registry by browsing still has to **join** it, which requires in-person alliance contact, but there is no stated channel for initiating that contact since the would-be member can’t post to the registry’s feeds — currently out-of-band by default. Newly recognized: this friction is doing decentralization work (it’s part of the in-person counter-gravity), so whatever contact mechanism is eventually built must not make registry-joining frictionless and recreate the relay-accretion gravity. The architecture doc states registries are alliances and that contents are browsable, but does not explicitly state that registry-joining is in-person like any alliance and has no separate discovery/push/pull mechanism — worth making explicit in the later doc pass.*
- **FED4.** Cross-mailbox causality commitments (credentials valid at presentation time, alliance composition at tally time, schism preserving in-progress operations, etc.): what breaks these commitments in deployed federated systems? *Status: open.*
- **FED5.** Hub-visible traffic analysis in E2EE group messaging: known transport-layer mitigations short of full onion routing, and the current state of the art for bounding what a blind-but-traffic-observing hub learns. *Status: open.*

## Transparency logs

- **LOG1.** Rust implementation path for Static-CT-API-style logs: existing crates vs. porting the Sunlight/TesseraCT patterns — what prior art exists? *Status: closed by audit (2026-06-12), converts to an architecture-doc fix.* The mature Static-CT ecosystem is Go, not Rust: Sunlight (Filippo/Let’s Encrypt), TesseraCT (Google, on the Tessera Go library), Trillian, plus Azul/Itko/CompactLog — and the 2024→2025 effort explicitly consolidated tooling around the Tessera Go library as the focal point. There is no mature Rust Static-CT implementation that was missed; the ecosystem genuinely lives in Go. This collides with Haven’s Rust crypto stack (OpenMLS, vodozemac), so the real resolution is an engineering decision, not a question for the transparency-dev community: run a Go Static-CT log (Tessera/Sunlight) as a separate service alongside the Rust core (the polyglot path the ecosystem expects and the likely answer), vs. Go-via-FFI, vs. a solo Rust port (which the ecosystem has not done). The architecture doc’s “specific Rust implementation path is pending investigation” framing is slightly wrong — it presupposes a Rust path the ecosystem answer doesn’t take — and should be corrected, in the later doc-revision pass, to the run-a-Go-log-as-a-service direction. (Design tension to note honestly when it’s folded in: this forces a third language into the stack — Elixir/Phoenix backend, Rust crypto core, now Go for the log — which the project would have preferred to avoid. The temptation under our-use-case optimization will be to fork one of the official Go logs, which re-incurs much of the maintenance cost a port would have; the discipline is to use the upstream as a service unmodified wherever possible and treat any fork as a real maintenance liability, not a free customization.) LOG2 (witness participation) and LOG3 (cost) are answerable from the ecosystem’s public material (active welcoming community; ~1.5 TB per six-month shard at WebPKI volumes, far above Haven’s) and are lurk-and-learn, not ask-blind. None of LOG1–3 needs to be put to the devs.
- **LOG2.** Witness-network participation for a non-WebPKI use case (instance equivocation defense, replica integrity attestation): policy or capacity concerns from witness operators; precedents beyond CT and Sigsum. *Status: open.*
- **LOG3.** Operational cost reality at Haven’s shape: many small logs vs. few large ones, and temporal sharding at sub-WebPKI scale. *Status: open.*

## Governance and civic practice

- **CIV1.** Has anyone validated governance-rule parameters (vote thresholds, schism thresholds, phase-dependent tuning) via agent-based simulation rather than intuition? We are considering building such a framework and would rather extend existing work. *Status: open.*
- **CIV2.** What kills deliberation platforms *after* adoption — practitioner post-mortems beyond the published record, and specifically whether governance-feature complexity contributed. *Status: resolved as background with one live design implication by audit (2026-06-12).* Prediction (governance absence, specifically schism) is partially right but not the headline killer. The most-cited post-adoption death is **participation/engagement collapse**: deliberation is costly, attracting people to deliberate is hard, and governance-heavy spaces tend toward one-off bursts then ghost-town. Schism-absence is a real but *different* failure mode (destructive fragmentation when there’s no internal exit), so keep schism — it’s the pressure valve against tearing-apart — but it doesn’t solve the more common participation-fatigue death. The counter-hypothesis (complexity kills) is confirmed in a specific, actionable form by PolicyKit’s own paper: manual governance burns out the small number of privileged people who do the work, sometimes driving core contributors out entirely; and decisively, **“most communities follow the default governance pattern made easy to adopt by their platform software”** — communities run on whatever the defaults are, because configuring custom governance is too costly for most. So complexity doesn’t kill by being *present*; it kills by being *mandatory or default-on*, taxing the median community and burning out the few who engage. **Design implication for the still-open complexity dial (the real value):** default-simple, opt-into-complex. Haven’s elaborate machinery (configurable thresholds, disclosure scopes, schism, the math-enabled apparatus) must ship with sane simple defaults a community can govern on *having configured nothing*, with complexity available but never required. This is the friction-as-ratchet / config-slider direction already in the civic-tech design notes — the evidence elevates it from UX nicety to load-bearing survival property. Two parts of Haven’s architecture already aim at the participation-cost death: distributed/representative governance (vs. PolicyKit’s burnout-prone privileged few) and the blind operator removed from moderation entirely. Good Metagov conversation (PolicyKit authors documented exactly this). General design direction is fixed and validated; the defaults discipline is the thing this finding should actively shape.
- **CIV3.** Membership-weighted vs. entity-weighted federated governance in practice: lived experience and observed gaming patterns. *Status: resolved into an accurate description + an open design comparison (2026-06-12; corrected against the governance doc after two wrong readings).* The actual mechanism has two levels, and earlier readings in this session (bicameralism; then “entity-eligibility floor, opposite of bicameralism”) both mis-grabbed the Foundation threshold and applied it to the whole structure. Correct statement:
  - **Alliance / general people-vote:** weight derives from individual humans — each human has 1.0 vote-mass split across their community memberships (a person in N communities contributes 1/N through each), flowing up *through* their community’s single representative, so a representative’s vote carries weight proportional to the deduplicated humans behind them. One seat per entity; the seat’s *weight* is proportional to constituency. The org runs its own internal election however it likes and its representative speaks for it — Haven never reaches inside. The design value: **no new democratic plumbing inside communities**; Haven weights the seat, the org fills it by its own process.
  - **Foundation Alliance:** the 0.01%/10,000 figure is an *eligibility threshold to hold a platform-governance seat at all*, and its primary reason is **MLS scaling** (cap composition at ~10,000 so the body stays a vanilla MLS group, no speculative “trees of MLS trees”), with aggregation-driving a documented *consequence*, not the primary motive. Within the capped body, representation is still **proportional to actual human constituents** — the threshold governs who’s in the room, not how proportional the weighting is.
  - The genuine direct-people-vote exception is the **instance-alliance level**, which uses one-share-per-community (federation verifiers must check signatures against composition; per-community-count tracking across federation is brittle). This is one of two honestly-documented **principle compromises forced by technical constraints**; the other is instance-alliance portability multisig. (Worth a short explicit “compromises forced by technical feasibility” list in the architecture or governance doc — naming them together is a credibility move consistent with the design-in-the-open methodology.)
    So the structure is **proportional-weighted representative democracy with chamber size bounded by MLS scaling**; no one’s vote-mass is discarded, it routes through a representative because the body can’t seat everyone directly — which is exactly the “gets the most people represented” goal.
    **Open design comparison (the live question, good for Metagov):** single-weighted-delegate (current plan) vs. parliamentary/proportional internal seating. Current design trades *internal-minority representation* for *institutional autonomy + protocol simplicity*: a single representative casts the org’s whole weighted bloc, so an org’s internal 60/40 split shows up as 100% the representative’s way — internal minorities are invisible at the platform level (the distortion proportional parliamentary systems exist to avoid). Parliamentary-style seating would surface internal pluralism but requires the platform to know each org’s *internal* vote distribution — invasive (breaks “orgs run themselves”) or gameable (self-reported), and it multiplies seats against the MLS cap. The trade is defensible and arguably correct for Haven specifically, because “communities are real and run themselves” is a core thesis; forcing internal-vote transparency to enable platform-level minority representation would violate it. Possible middle path worth exploring in the apportionment/parliamentary literature: orgs *optionally* seating multiple weighted representatives to express internal pluralism if they choose, without the platform mandating visibility. The value of this literature is **implementation, not justification** (apportionment methods — D’Hondt, Sainte-Laguë, Huntington-Hill — and their known paradoxes are the same problems Haven’s weighting will hit). **Design finding (stands, independent of the corrections):** one-member-one-vote’s turnout/quorum-capture failure mode (“a dedicated faction that always shows up controls low-turnout votes”) is *amplified* by attendance-anchoring — but per the founder, attendance-rewarding is mission-aligned (encouraging in-person interaction is the whole point), so this is a deliberate feature with a residual pathology, and the sharpened open question is “how to keep the mission-aligned benefit (attendance matters) without the pathology (small dedicated factions dominate low-turnout decisions).” Three aligned consequences of the structure the founder noted: (a) pressure to organize into alliances (sub-threshold entities must aggregate for Foundation representation) — sound; (b) soft pressure against schism for large stable bodies (schism fragments people-weight) — real but double-edged, must discourage *frivolous* schism without becoming a *trap* that discourages *legitimate* schism (keep first-class schism cheap); (c) attendance rewarded, which is mission-aligned. Belongs in the governance doc’s open considerations, not outside review.
- **CIV4.** Organizations that tie governance weight to verified attendance (unions, co-ops, congregations): what proxy and absentee mechanisms proved necessary, and what participation discount do disabled, homebound, and dispersed members actually experience? *Status: open, resolved into a design position by audit (2026-06-12; corrected within the session).* Prediction confirmed: high-membership-pressure institutions (unions) made attendance-weight work, low-pressure ones wouldn’t need it. Real institutions use an **excused-absence pattern** (UAW: a member “may be excused from attendance for good cause,” judged by a designated body) rather than transferable proxy. **Important correction made during the audit:** an initial reading held that excused-absence-by-vouching is sybil-safe for Haven because it is community-anchored. That is wrong. The in-person component is *what makes vouching sybil-resistant in the first place*; a community attesting an absent member’s “excused presence” without any physical verification is just the community asserting a member exists — exactly the inflation vector attendance-anchoring exists to close. The union pattern is safe *for unions* only because their members are externally real (employed at the workplace, dues checked off); Haven lacks that external anchor, so copying excused-absence-by-vouching would reopen the sybil backdoor. The legal framing was also overstated: LMRDA / 29 CFR 452 obligations bind *unions* for their own statutory elections, not Haven (infrastructure) and not as a general principle; ADA/ARIA-type obligations attach to specific covered entities, not a protocol, and much is voluntary-standard. A community with statutory-election duties handles its own compliance and decides whether Haven fits. **Resolution (decouple participation from sybil-weight):** absentee/remote members get full community membership and participation (read, take part, vote in the community’s internal sense); what attendance-anchoring withholds from non-attendance is contribution to the *Foundation-level sybil-resisted attendance-attested count* — because that count’s entire value is that it can’t be faked, and only verified physical presence carries that property. This is not disenfranchisement: it’s that the high-assurance governance tier requires the high-assurance signal. The accessibility cost is owned honestly rather than engineered away: a community heavy with non-attending members carries less Foundation-level weight than its headcount suggests, and that consequence is stated plainly rather than patched with a mechanism that would reopen the attack. Population distinction still holds: **group 1** (people seeking non-in-person community) — “monoculture is the enemy; not Haven’s fit” is correct; **group 2** (members of Haven communities who can’t physically attend) — fully included in community life, simply not contributing the attendance-attested sybil-weight they can’t generate the sybil-resistant way. This is load-bearing on the attendance-anchoring work: the correct companion to attendance-weight is *not* a vouched excused-absence credential (which reopens the backdoor) but an honest separation between participation and sybil-weight.
- **NEW5 (research).** Cost-of-attack analysis of attendance-anchored governance counting: the claim is that anchoring counting to attested in-person attendance converts sybil attacks from key-minting into operating-real-humans-through-real-events, with tenure vesting forcing the slow version. This is a mechanism-design claim that deserves security-economics scrutiny, not just cryptographic scrutiny. *Status: open.*

## Usable security and trust & safety

- **NEW1 (research).** Known failure modes of in-person QR key ceremonies at community scale, and of asking non-expert users to make per-message disclosure and escalation decisions (key-reveal, report-or-not). The design leans heavily on ceremonies; the usable-security literature studies exactly where ceremonies fail. *Status: open, sharpened from vague to a specific design principle by audit (2026-06-12).* The “in-person join rides on existing social behavior” intuition is strongly validated by the PGP autopsy: the web of trust died precisely because key-signing was a *novel ritual with no host behavior to attach to* (a 2018 study found only 0.3% of public PGP keys had any signatures; “nobody does this… it asked too much of ordinary people”; “ordinary people will trust anything that looks like a PGP key”). Haven’s join attaches a credential to a gathering people already attend and a contact-exchange they already perform — the difference between adoption and the 0.3%. But the literature delivers a counterintuitive warning that reshapes the design lever: the dangerous failure is *transparency*, not difficulty — when key management was made automatic/invisible (Garfinkel & Miller; Sheng et al.; Pwm), users couldn’t tell whether the security action had happened, failed to read indicators, and *lost trust because the system was too smooth*. So the lever is not merely “clear messaging / make it easy” but **make each security-critical moment legible as a distinct, perceivable act the user experiences themselves completing, without making it burdensome.** Sorting the worry-list: (1) the **attendance ritual is the highest-risk ceremony** — it has the least existing behavior to ride on (joining rides on contact-exchange; attendance-attestation rides on nothing people already do) and is the most tempting to make invisible (just detect presence), which is exactly the transparency trap; too automatic and users don’t perceive the governance-critical act, too manual and it’s a novel ritual that won’t adopt. Load-bearing on the sybil/attendance-anchoring work. (2) **multisig / democracy levers** are infrequent and role-concentrated (representatives, not every member), so they fit the “motivated/trained expert ceremony” case the literature says *can* work — a training-and-rehearsal problem, not a mass-usability one. (3) **report-or-not** is a known flow from existing platforms; the novel variable is that the reporter knows who they’re reporting (could cut either way — social inhibition vs. accountability), an empirical pilot question, not a literature gap. Stays a research/pilot-calibration question, now targeted; strong SOUPS-community conversation.
- **NEW2.** Case-load realism for volunteer jury moderation: the franking pipeline produces verified-evidence packages for juries and councils — what evidence-package design and case-flow shape has and hasn’t worked under real abuse volume? *Status: open, reframed from a volume question to an interpersonal-conflict question by audit (2026-06-12).* The decisive finding reorders the prediction: **volunteer moderators quit from interpersonal conflict and burnout, NOT from traumatic content** the way commercial moderators do (Wikipedia-admin study: volunteers quit “due to interpersonal conflict, burnout, and unsupportive leadership, rather than traumatic content as seen in commercial moderation”; Schöpke-Gonzalez names the top quit-causes as “too little available time” and “struggles with other moderators”). So the commercial-moderation trauma-at-volume nightmare is largely a *commercial* problem Haven’s design sheds (blind operator, community rules, no global firehose), and the volume-reduction claims (no anonymity, no firehose-brigading, vouching self-filters, sensibility-clustering lowers inter-community conflict) are real but were aimed at the secondary threat. The *primary* volunteer-moderator killer is interpersonal — and Haven’s “everyone knows each other / no anonymity” property, which reduces bad behavior, simultaneously *intensifies* the hardest part: adjudicating a dispute between people you know is heavier emotional labor than an anonymous mod banning a stranger (the NEW1 “knowing who you’re reporting” variable, cutting toward difficulty). **The jury design is a strong structural answer to the actual threat, better than the volume argument that was made for it:** rotation prevents the uneven-load quit-cause (“some mods do more”); a jury-as-body means no one absorbs interpersonal conflict or makes the enemy-making decision *alone* (diffusing “targeted for their decisions”); collective handling is exactly the team-based, trauma-informed support the literature recommends. So the load-bearing jury virtues are distribution and shared-load (not volume). **Design implication (the real output):** evidence-package and case-flow design should target *low-volume, high-interpersonal-stakes, relationship-embedded* disputes — conflict-of-interest recusal when a juror knows the parties, de-escalation scaffolding, emotional backing for jurors — NOT high-volume content-triage (the commercial model Haven doesn’t face). The sensibility-clustering double-edge is confirmed: it lowers inter-community throughput but raises intra-community drift (identity-clustered communities generate intense *internal* boundary-policing labor — the AAPI-subreddit work — and a community clustered around something harmful polices it less, the CR-F6 problem at community scale). Strong T&S-community conversation.
- **NEW3.** In-person-rooted joining and accessibility: the in-person gate is a deliberate sybil-resistance choice with a real cost for disabled, rural, and immunocompromised people. What accommodation patterns preserve the gate’s properties while reducing the exclusion? *Status: resolved by design reasoning (2026-06-12).* The join vouch can come *to* the person — a member physically goes to the homebound or mobility-limited prospective member, preserving the in-person sybil anchor because someone was bodily present with them. So *joining* is accessible: presence comes to you. *Attendance-weight* cannot be delivered the same way, because attendance is a claim about being at a specific event in a specific place — that location-specificity is the unforgeable signal, and it can’t be brought to someone’s living room without becoming forgeable. One principle (physical presence is the sybil anchor) applied to two different claims, not two inconsistent positions: join-presence can travel, event-presence cannot. Consistent with the CIV4 resolution (full participation for absentee members; attendance-attested Foundation-weight requires actual attendance). The accessibility cost is owned honestly: the homebound can fully join and participate, contributing to everything except the location-specific attendance count.
- **Shared jury pools as a core alliance function** (design direction surfaced 2026-06-12; to fold into settled docs). Distinct from what the docs already have: architecture (entity model) and features §10.5 say alliances can “share moderation decisions, shunning lists, credential trust” — i.e. propagate *outcomes/rules*. The unwritten piece is sharing the *adjudicators*: empaneling a community’s jury (or part of it) from **allied communities’ members**, so the deciders are bound by shared alliance rules but external to the reporting community. Double duty against two audit findings: (a) *intra-community drift (CR-F6 / clustering tail)* — a drifting community polices its own boundary less because the jury shares the drift; allied jurors who don’t share it mean a report still has consequences, the concrete governance-layer implementation of the only closer CR-F6 identified for the all-malicious-group gap, at community scale; (b) *interpersonal moderation labor (NEW2)* — an allied juror is bound by shared rules but not personally entangled with the parties, the outside-arbiter/recusal mechanism that relieves NEW2’s “ruling on someone you know is heavier” problem (venue-change logic). Honest cost: weakens rule-ownership (a community judged by its own people is part of the pitch; outside jurors match local norms less perfectly) — so opt-in and configurable, likely per case type (routine cases internal where rule-ownership matters; conflict-of-interest cases and appeals external where neutrality matters), the constitutional-precommitment/config-slider pattern again. Positioning: shared *adjudication across community boundaries* is only implementable where communities are first-class federated entities with portable credentials and shared rules — a Discord bot can’t empanel jurors from another server, so bots/plugins on centralized platforms structurally can’t do this; it’s a Haven-unique differentiator, not just a feature. Fold-in targets: architecture (extend the alliance shared-moderation line to shared adjudication); features §10.5 and §14.13 (configurable allied jury-pool empanelment); whole-system-governance (anti-drift/anti-capture mechanism alongside the competability thesis).

## Operations and economics

- **NEW4.** What does running a federated-network instance actually cost, in money and hours, at various scales — and what made operators quit? *Status: open, not pre-send-urgent, framing sharpened by audit (2026-06-12).* Not urgent because there are no other operators until federation (Phase 4); this is Phase-4 background, lurk-and-learn from fediverse-admin experience. The audit confirms the load-bearing assumption (operator-plurality is achievable) is *more* defensible for Haven than for Mastodon, for a specific citable reason: fediverse operator burnout is **moderation-dominated, not infrastructure-dominated** (the mastodon.technology shutdown, the FT instance closure, Garrison’s “Mastodon Is Doomed,” and Nick Johnson’s sustainability analysis all center the legal/emotional moderation load and the hosting-economics-corrupts-moderation failure mode). Haven’s blind operator has no moderation role by default, so it sheds the dominant cost category — and structurally cannot have the worst failure mode (moderation choices driven by hosting economics). Bare hosting is already cheap (~$0.0085/registered account, ~$0.041/MAU), so affordability isn’t the wall. The transferable pains are the **operational skill floor** and **spike/abuse exposure** (mastodon.technology died from a traffic spike requiring emergency sysadmin skill a volunteer lacked; spam attacks have weaponized infrastructure cost into surprise four-figure bills). So the sharpened question is “infrastructure-operations costs and pain points, filtered from moderation — specifically the skill floor and spike/abuse exposure,” and the design work that makes plurality real is not cost reduction but **collapsing the skill floor and absorbing spikes** (turnkey deployment; graceful degradation; and the FED3 replica/read-only-external-surface model, which routes viral spikes to replicas rather than the small operator’s authoritative server — the structural answer to the exact failure that killed mastodon.technology). Surfaced design decision: **the certified-operator directory’s default ordering must be randomized or locality-based, never popularity-ranked** — this is the instance-layer counter-gravity against the atproto “anyone can, nobody does” consolidation (FED3), the analog of the in-person gate for registries. Staged-centralization position (founder’s): deliberate centralization early for non-technical-adopter friction-minimization, engineered diffusion later via the certified directory; plausible but not asserted as fact.
