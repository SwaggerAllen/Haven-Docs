# Why design the whole thing before building any of it?
 
> *Haven is a federated, end-to-end-encrypted platform built around communities instead of individuals — civic infrastructure, not another social network. This is a journal about designing and building it, in the open.*
 
*Haven — The Slow Play, Part 2*
 
*← The Slow Play, Part 1: [Where they can’t follow](PASTE-POST-4-URL)*
 
It does look like the wrong way to build something. A person sits down to fix social media and, instead of building the smallest thing that works and seeing if anyone wants it, designs a messaging protocol, a credential system, a federation model, a constitution, a foundation, and a company — on paper, all at once, none of it running. It reads less like someone about to ship than like someone in love with the completeness of a plan, adding rooms to a cathedral while the foundation sits unpoured.
 
## The plan, in phases
 
The build comes in phases, each one a platform a real community could actually use, with later phases adding reach rather than fixing what earlier ones got wrong. A pilot to start — one instance, one community, a deliberately cut-down core — growing into the full single-community platform. Then multiple communities on that instance, with the full set of governance and membership tools. Then the money-touching parts — dues, tickets, physical member cards — which drag in payment processing and legal work the earlier phases didn’t need. Then federation: many instances, run by different people, talking to each other, communities able to pick up and move between them. Then maturity, where the plugin system comes online and communities can make the platform whatever they need it to be. Five layers, each resting on the ones below it.
 
The pieces are as modular as I can make them — clean boundaries, separable concerns, the usual discipline. But modular doesn’t mean independent. Across those clean boundaries they still constrain each other, hard. The credential system decides what the governance layer is allowed to assume about who’s a real person; the federation model decides what the messaging layer has to expose to route between servers; the requirement that abuse reports stay verifiable reaches all the way back down into which encryption gets used at the bottom. Design any one of these alone and you don’t get a working piece you can keep — you get a piece built against assumptions the next layer turns out to violate, and you tear it out.
 
And the cost of getting the foundations wrong doesn’t stay put. It compounds. It isn’t one bad call low in the stack — one you’d just eat. It’s that a handful of them, scattered through the base, each surfacing late, each forcing a refactor between phases that tears through code the earlier phases were supposed to have settled. Get the addressing scheme and the identity primitive and the ordering model a little wrong, discover it once the cryptography sits on top, and you don’t fix three things — you rebuild the cryptography, which forces the federation layer that assumed it, which forces the plugin interface that assumed that. The refactors chain through the same layers, phase after phase, instead of staying local.
 
That’s the kind of thing that adds years to a project that’s already long — years to build something this size even with every call made right, and more years getting real institutions to trust it, on a timeline I don’t set. Those are the costs of the work itself. The refactor cascade is a cost on top of them, self-inflicted, and the one category of delay I can actually prevent. I can’t hurry the cryptographers or the institutions. I can decline to build three layers on a base I haven’t checked. Spending design time now, which is cheap, to keep from burning build years later, which are not, is the whole trade.
 
There’s a sharper version of the objection than the one I just answered — not “build one piece and ship it,” but “build one thin thread through all the layers at once.” One message, sent by one credentialed person, in one community, on one instance — not to ship, but to expose the couplings, because the couplings between layers are exactly what a paper design gets wrong, and running code is what drags them into the light. A design that has never executed has unexamined couplings too; they’re just invisible until something runs. That much is true: I can’t see every coupling from here, and some only a running system will show me.
 
But the objection has a false premise — that designing first means not building. It doesn’t. Running code will teach me things no amount of reasoning could, and I’ll write plenty of it; the pilot is exactly that, a real thread through real layers. What design-first settles isn’t whether to build, but what to do first. A coupling you catch by reasoning about the whole structure costs a redraft. The same coupling, hit in running code after three layers already rest on the assumption it violates, costs the cascade — the rebuild that chains up through everything stacked on top. So designing the whole thing first refuses one specific thing — building on couplings I haven’t reasoned through — and not prototyping itself, which I’ll do plenty of. The reasoning is cheap; the rebuild is not.
 
The deeper reason isn’t about cost at all. A high-level design is free. Anyone can write “communities instead of individuals, federated, private, governed by their members” — it costs nothing and commits to nothing, and you cannot tell from it whether the author has solved anything or just arranged some appealing words. What this platform is trying to do is serve three things that genuinely pull against each other: what a society needs from civic infrastructure (that it can’t be captured and turned), what a community needs (governance shaped like the way it already runs itself), and what a person needs (privacy, and not being the product). Those conflict, constantly, at specific points. Anonymous voting versus one-person-one-vote. Abuse reporting versus deniability. Exit rights versus the stability of the thing you’re exiting. You do not get to claim you’ve reconciled them by gesturing at a vision. You only get to claim it by showing, at each collision, which tradeoff you chose and what it cost.
 
So the detail is what makes the design something a person can actually check — and disagree with — not a symptom of overplanning at all. A vaguer design wouldn’t be more disciplined; it would be less honest, hiding every hard conflict under an abstraction smooth enough that no one could see where it was doing the real work. The completeness is what makes it falsifiable.
 
One failure mode is worth naming, because it’s the one design-first projects actually die of: the design that never ends, the cathedral always a room short of done. What keeps this from becoming that is a finish line I can state. The design is done when the coupled whole is specified well enough to hand a reviewer and build against, with the genuinely open problems marked open rather than smoothed over. That’s a weaker claim than “everything is decided” — the cryptographic residuals wait on experts, the pilot waits on a real community — but those are named gaps, not unfinished design. By that standard I’m essentially there. What remains is getting the design checked and starting to build against it, not more designing.
 
## What I’m not designing yet
 
None of which means everything is designed. The opposite — the exception is as deliberate as the rule.
 
The pilot — Phase zero, the very first thing that gets built — is the least specified part of the entire design. The full single-community platform, Phase one, is too big to be a real first step, so the pilot is a deliberately cut-down slice of it, several of its features left as “basic version of X” on purpose. I haven’t specified it in detail because doing so right now would be the same mistake as building piecemeal, run in reverse — not because I lost steam at the part that matters most. Two things I don’t yet have would determine those specifics, and guessing ahead of them is how you design the wrong thing confidently. The first is the finished design itself — until the coupled whole is settled, I don’t know all the constraints the pilot has to satisfy. The second is a real first organization. The pilot isn’t built for “a community” in the abstract; it’s built for the actual one that goes first, shaped to what it actually needs. Designing it before that organization exists means substituting my guess about a community’s life for the community’s life. The right move is to design what’s coupled and knowable and refuse to specify what depends on a contact with reality I haven’t made yet.
 
Which is the point where the work stops being mine to do alone, and stops being technical at all.
 
Getting that first organization is not a design problem. It’s the work of finding a real institution, sitting with how it actually operates, and earning enough trust to be allowed inside the part of its life it doesn’t hand to strangers. It’s a different discipline from building the protocol, done in different rooms. The blank where the pilot’s details should be is work that hasn’t happened yet — because it can’t happen at a desk, and because it’s the half of this I’m least able to do alone.
 
That’s the methodology, both halves of it: design whole what’s coupled, refuse to specify what reality hasn’t taught you yet. It answers how the thing gets designed and how it gets built. It says nothing about the question the size of all this keeps raising — what happens to a project meant to outlast a decade when, right now, every part of it runs through one person. How does a thing like this survive the person who started it? That’s the next one.
 
— Allen
