# We built the internet as if every man were an island
 
*Introducing Haven, Part 2*
 
*← Part 1: [What if the problem isn’t the algorithm?](PASTE-POST-1-URL)*
 
No man is an island, the line goes — and yet almost everything we’ve built to be social online assumes the opposite. The account is the atom: a single person, standing alone, broadcasting outward. Communities, where they exist at all, are a convenience laid over a pile of those atoms — a subreddit, a group chat, a server — and the individual underneath is the real, durable thing the system is built around. But that’s backwards from how people actually live. The person was never the whole unit; they were always a piece of something larger, and the something larger is where most of what matters to them actually happens. So here’s the question: what would you build if you took that seriously — if the community, not the person, were the basic thing?
 
Haven inverts the usual arrangement, and everything else hangs on it. A community is a first-class object with its own identity, its own rules, and its own continuity, and it does not depend on any particular member to keep existing. People are members *of* communities; the community is the thing that persists. That sounds like a small reframing. It changes almost everything downstream — and what follows is the outline, not the whole of it. There’s a lot of machinery, and most of it is what the rest of this journal is for; this is enough to see whether it hangs together.
 
## A few real things, and how they relate
 
The design runs on a small set of entities. I’ll introduce four, because four is enough to see how they fit, and the interesting part is less the entities themselves than how they relate. The first two are *social* — they’re about people and groups. The second two are *infrastructure* — they’re about machines. Keeping those straight matters.
 
A **community** is what I just described: a group of people with shared rules and governance, with its own cryptographic identity, separate from any member’s. This is the social and human unit — the congregation, the neighborhood association, the co-op, the parent-teacher group. It’s the thing the platform exists to serve.
 
An **alliance** is communities choosing to cooperate — sharing rules, recognizing each other’s members, pooling moderation, keeping a common calendar. It’s the digital version of a denomination, a regional network, a set of sister organizations. It’s a *social* relationship, decided by the communities involved through their own governance.
 
An **instance** is the server that hosts communities — the infrastructure. And here’s the first idea I’d call a design decision rather than an obvious choice: an instance is split into two parts that most systems fuse together. There’s the **operator** — whoever has root on the machine, pays the hosting bill, and carries the legal responsibility — and there’s a **governance layer**, which holds authority over policy and, crucially, holds the instance’s durable identity. That governance layer is itself an alliance — recall that an alliance is just communities banding together under shared governance, and the communities sharing a server are exactly that: a group governing the thing they have in common. The seam is the point — a social object governing a machine — one of several places the design inherits, from the way communities already work, something other platforms have to build from scratch.
 
Why split them? From the fediverse to the major networks, operators end up with outsized say over what can be said. Both the left and the right accuse Facebook of discriminating against them — which is what you get when an operator has that much discretionary power over speech and almost nothing checks it. We don’t have to live with unaccountable public forums. Some of that accountability is cryptographic: an operator can be barred from *forging* a community’s decisions or *impersonating* the instance’s identity, because those rest on keys the operator doesn’t hold. But crypto can’t stop someone with root from censoring, dropping traffic, or freezing the machine — and the honest answer to *that* power isn’t prevention; it’s the next paragraph. I love my nerds. But being able to run a server does not confer the wisdom to run a community. Those are two different aptitudes, and keeping them structurally separate is what insulates each from the other.
 
Because the durable identity lives with the governance layer and not the operator, a community is never trapped on a machine. You can’t evict someone who holds root — but you don’t have to. If an operator goes bad, the community reconstitutes elsewhere and carries its identity with it; the bad operator is left holding inert hardware, not a hostage. Root can make trouble in the meantime — drop your traffic, freeze the box — but it can’t *keep* you, and “you can always leave without losing yourself” is most of what accountability needs. And the same property buys something practical. On the fediverse today, whether a community exists, and whether it survives, depends on one or a few people staying interested enough to keep a server running. That’s a barrier to entry for the community, and a tax on the admin, whose workload grows with every community they take on. Separating the community from the machine and making identity portable removes both: a community doesn’t need its own technical caretaker to exist, and a technical person can support as many or as few communities as they like without standing up something separate for each one.
 
**Federation** is the other infrastructure piece, and keeping it separate from alliance is the second deliberate decision. Federation is just *servers interoperating* — one talking to another so a member on one can participate in a community on the other. It’s plumbing. It carries no social meaning by itself. Most platforms smear these two ideas together, so that “these servers talk to each other” gets read as “these groups are affiliated” — but those are different facts. Alliance is who you’re affiliated with; federation is which machines happen to be exchanging packets. They can line up. They don’t have to, and the design is cleaner for not assuming they do.
 
There’s more — how members verify themselves, how communities get discovered, how a person carries a primary affiliation — but those are later posts, not part of this skeleton.
 
## What falls out
 
Two things become natural once the community is the unit, and they’re the kind of thing that’s hard to bolt onto a system built the other way.
 
The first is **schism** — a community splitting in two. Schism happens in real communities; it always has. The problem today is that splitting means *leaving*: a faction has to decamp to a different platform or different software, and the moment they do, interoperability breaks — the people who left and the people who stayed can no longer reach each other at all. Think of every time a community has fractured and half of it fled to a rival service: the disagreement was about one thing, but the split severed *everything*, and a soft divide hardened into two sealed bubbles that can’t talk. Haven treats a split as a *designed operation*. The dissenting group can fork into a new community and is meant to carry its history, its credentials, its accumulated trust with it, rather than starting from nothing — and the break stays at the level of the actual disagreement. No new hardware, no new software, no severing the people on either side from each other. The exact mechanism is one of the harder problems I’m still working: making a clean split *provably* clean, when the parties may not trust each other, is genuinely difficult. The hard part isn’t even schism-specific — it’s the general question of how order and history survive between parties who don’t trust each other. But the design is settled in outline: a community can divide without it being a catastrophe.
 
The second is how people **join**, and it’s where the whole approach to bad actors comes from. You join a community through an in-person connection — a real-world handoff from someone already inside. That pushes people into the world; it makes the connection real. And it makes *not* being real expensive: the cost of infiltration scales with *people*, not with server cycles — flooding a community should take roughly as many real human beings as the accounts you’d want to fake, at the speed of actual people actually meeting, rather than a script spinning up a thousand identities from a basement. That’s the whole anti-abuse story, and it isn’t a separate system bolted on top — it falls out of how joining works.
 
I should be plain about what that costs. An in-person, vouched handoff is about the steepest on-ramp in consumer software, and it means Haven grows at the speed of people actually meeting each other — which is to say slowly, maybe the slowest of any way you could choose to grow. I’m choosing to pay that, because in-person vouching is the only way I’ve found to make sybil resistance scale without an arms race, and I’m betting that enough people want what it buys — even if they don’t yet have the word for it — that it works out anyway, on a longer clock than a normal launch runs on. The cost is smallest where I mean to start: communities that already meet in person, where the handoff is something they do already.
 
And the gate does a second job I didn’t design it for: it aims. The same friction that’s prohibitive for a diffuse crowd of internet strangers is nearly free for a congregation on Sunday or a co-op at its monthly assembly — the gathering the handoff needs already happens every week. The constraint that slows growth is the same mechanism that points the platform at exactly the communities it’s for.
 
Making that cost hold up under a hostile insider — binding one human to one identity even when whoever’s vouching is corrupt — is one of the harder open problems, and I’ll get to it. But the friction that makes a community feel real is the same friction that keeps the bots and the scammers out.
 
## What’s deliberately not here
 
A couple of absences are worth naming as choices, because a reader expecting a social network will notice them, and I’d rather they read as intent than oversight.
 
There is no global feed, and individual broadcast — one person publishing to the world at large — isn’t a first-class operation. People speak through the communities they’re part of; with the community as the unit, there’s no global “everyone” for a feed to aggregate in the first place. (My bartender friend from the last post doesn’t need to broadcast to the whole internet; he needs his regulars to know which nights he’s working — which is a community knowing a thing, not a man shouting into a feed.) And it buys two things. First, you always know your audience — your message isn’t a bottle thrown in the ocean that Crazy Uncle Richard fishes out three weeks later. Second, things spread *slowly*: you might get asked to leave a community for behaving badly, but you’re far less likely to be canceled in front of the entire internet. It isn’t safer for everyone, either — sometimes the whole world’s attention is the only thing that holds power to account, and a small room is exactly where some harm hides. What it is, is a relief from a particular exhaustion: you don’t have to perform for the entire world, all at once, all the time. Most of what people actually want to say is meant for a room, not a stadium, and a system built around rooms lets them say it that way.
 
If the only thing you think is wrong with social media is the ranking algorithm, this is probably where you decide Haven isn’t for you. That’s all right. It isn’t built to be a better feed. It’s built to be a different thing.
 
## A few ideas, reused — and why
 
On most platforms, stopping fake accounts and infiltration is a technical arms race — captchas, phone numbers, behavioral detection, machine learning, all of it trying to guess whether there’s a real distinct person behind an account, and all of it losing ground as the fakes get better. Haven mostly doesn’t play that game, because it inherits its sybil resistance from how the communities already work. Joining one isn’t filling out a form — it’s being vouched for, in person, by someone already inside, who is themselves accountable for the vouch. Membership is grounded in real-world institutions and locality. To flood a Haven community with fakes you don’t need to beat an algorithm; you need real humans willing to physically show up and vouch for your fakes, and to spend their own standing when the fakes misbehave. The cost of an attack is paid in genuine human relationships and reputations, not server cycles — which is a cost the attacker can’t drive to zero, because it isn’t a technical quantity.
 
That’s not a clever mechanism I designed. Human communities have always resisted infiltration this way — you don’t get to be a trusted member of a congregation or a neighborhood association by registering; you get there by showing up and being known, over time, by people who stake something on you. I modeled how communities already establish trust, and the resistance came with it. The same is true of how the pieces fit together — an instance’s governance is just an alliance of the communities sharing the server; a registry (a directory of communities) is just an alliance whose purpose is listing its members — because the institutions these are modeled on already nest and recombine on their own. Build on what communities already do, and a lot of what looks like it should be hard comes cheaper than it would otherwise.
 
It doesn’t come free, though, and it’s worth being precise about where it doesn’t. When communities ally and recognize each other’s members, an alliance is only as sybil-resistant as its most lax member — that part is real. What stops it from being the open free-for-all of unrestricted federation is that alliances aren’t open: a community has to be deliberately invited, by communities whose members have actually met, so the same in-person throttling that guards one community also scopes how far its trust travels. Local first, shared second, and weaker the further out it reaches — which is why whom a community allies with is a deliberate decision, made with its own tolerance for risk.
 
And that’s the bet underneath the whole design. The hard problem in something like this is trust — knowing who’s real, who belongs, who to believe. The usual instinct is to treat that as a problem to solve from scratch in software, with the humans as the part that keeps going wrong. The bet here runs the other way: communities already have trust. They’ve had it for as long as there have been communities — in how they let people in, vouch for each other, decide things, and part ways. Build the system to fit those mechanisms instead of replacing them, and you don’t have to manufacture trust from nothing. You inherit it. Most of what looks novel here is just that bet applied over and over — don’t invent the trust, model the thing that already has it.
 
It’s also why the design comes out simple in form even though the cryptography underneath it isn’t. (Simple in form is not the same as solved — the cryptography that has to hold it all up is where the hard, unfinished work lives, and that’s what the next post is about.) We’re not confining people into a new paradigm, or fighting what comes naturally. We’re mirroring what communities already do — which is also why the abstractions are flexible, because the organizations they mirror are flexible. They’re shaped to hold communities as they actually are, as faithfully as I could manage.
 
Which is what the next post is about: what’s solid, what isn’t, and the handful of hard problems I haven’t cracked.
