# Where they can’t follow
 
> *Haven is a federated, end-to-end-encrypted platform built around communities instead of individuals — civic infrastructure, not another social network. This is a journal about designing and building it, in the open.*
 
*Haven — The Slow Play, Part 1*
 
*← Introducing Haven, Part 3: [Where this stands, and what I’m stuck on](PASTE-POST-3-URL)*
 
What makes infrastructure worth relying on is that it’s still there after you’ve stopped paying attention to it, and that’s the one property you can’t demonstrate quickly. You earn it by lasting. So a thing like this is judged less by whether it’s a good idea than by whether it survives.
 
The most basic threat to that survival is also the most external. Before the cryptography has to work, before anyone has to trust me, there has to be ground here that isn’t already taken — and ground the people who could take it won’t. So start there: is there room for this to exist at all, or does something bigger just fill the space before it can?
 
Start with the fact that the thing works. Front Porch Forum has been running in Vermont since 2000. It reaches something like a quarter of a million people, in a state of just 640,000 — on a product that could hardly be less like the rest of the web: posts go out once a day, in a digest, which makes a flame war about as satisfying as arguing by postcard. Every message is read by a human before it publishes. There’s no venture money; it’s a public benefit corporation built to sustain itself and not much more. During Vermont’s floods it became the place neighbors found each other. Twenty-five years, calm, local, mission intact, paying its own way.
 
It’s the proof that the demand is real. Locality-based civic software isn’t something I’m hypothesizing — it’s existed and worked for a quarter century. The basic wager, that communities rooted in a real place will use software built for them, is not the uncertain part.
 
It also shows where the model tops out, and the ceiling is the instructive part, because it’s the thing Haven exists to get past. Front Porch Forum works because it *is* the trusted operator. The moderation, the norms, the calm all rest on two decades of the founders’ standing in their own community — which is lovely, and which doesn’t transfer. You can’t copy that reputation to Ohio, which is why the thing is bound to one state and one family.
 
And even if you could, the model scales like a payroll — every new state needs its own hired team of readers. Haven moderates nothing at the center: each community carries its own small moderation load, in proportion to its own conversation, which is how a mesh of communities can grow past one state when Front Porch Forum, by its own math, can’t.
 
The quality comes from humans reading every message, which means the operator, and any future owner, any acquirer, any subpoena, can read every message too.
 
Two ceilings, and underneath they’re a single one: the whole thing rests on one trusted operator. That’s what makes it non-portable, and what makes it readable. For a Vermont town, none of this is a flaw to fix — Front Porch Forum is the right answer there, and Haven would be a worse one. Haven’s machinery only earns its cost where operator-independence or pseudonymity are essential. But where they are, the question is whether you can keep everything Front Porch Forum proved while removing the one operator it all rests on.
 
You might wonder, if the ground is real, why something enormous hasn’t taken it already. Not because it couldn’t — because there’s nothing in it for it. These companies run on attention: the product is engagement, and the revenue is whatever that engagement can be sold to. A platform built to *not* capture attention — no ads, no feed pulling you back, no growth past the size a community actually is — is worthless by every number they grade themselves on. They could build it. It would just be the worst-performing product they ever shipped, by every number they grade themselves on — which is the best reason to think they won’t bother.
 
But the giants were never the real competition. What a community actually chooses between isn’t Haven and Facebook. It’s Haven and the church-management software the congregation already pays for, or the Discord server that’s free and works fine, or the group chat everyone’s already in. Those are good, and they’re here, and “as smooth as Discord” is a reason to stay on Discord. Matching them on the basics — events, directory, dues, calendar — is the price of being allowed to make an argument at all, not the argument itself. If switching is going to cost a community anything, the thing it switches to has to do something the thing it has cannot.
 
Here is the thing they cannot do, and it’s structural, not a matter of effort. Take Planning Center, the gold standard in church software. It was bootstrapped, never took venture money, and in 2024 its founder put it under a foundation to keep it permanently not-for-sale. Mission-first, won’t sell out; they got there first and they mean it. On the surface it even looks like what Haven is trying to be — the place a congregation keeps its directory, its giving, its calendar, its scheduling. But it’s built for the church office, not the congregation. You manage members, track attendance, process giving, send announcements out to people. What you can’t do is hold a conversation: it’s one-way, staff to members, broadcast only. That limit is forced, not an oversight. Because the operator can read everything on its servers, it’s liable for whatever members say to each other, and hosting member-to-member speech would mean moderating it — at the scale of human review Front Porch Forum staffs and a software company has no way to fund. So it doesn’t offer it. The architecture that lets it read everything is the architecture that keeps it mute. Planning Center did everything right and still can’t cross this line, because the line isn’t about will or money or mission. It’s structural.
 
And it’s a trap, not a choice — Front Porch Forum could in principle abandon its readable model; a vendor whose product is the readable database cannot.
 
None of this is special to churches. It’s true of any tool built to administer a community rather than to be one — the back-office a nonprofit runs on, the portal a property manager logs into, the app a school pushes to parents. They hold the data, so they carry the liability, so they stay broadcast-only. The whole category is trapped on the same side of the same wall.
 
Haven is the same property turned over. The operator can’t read anything — so the operator can’t be the moderator, can’t be liable for the speech, can’t be the one deciding what’s allowed. Which means moderation and governance have to live inside the community, with the people who belong to it, because once the operator is blind there’s nowhere else for them to live. “Communities govern themselves” is less a principle I chose than the only thing left once the operator can’t see in.
 
That’s the ground the incumbents can’t follow onto — and not because they lack the engineers. To get here they’d have to stop being able to read their users’ data, and being able to read their users’ data is what their product is. An incumbent that went operator-blind wouldn’t be competing with Haven; it would be tearing down the thing it already sells in order to become something it has no reason to want to be. Nothing here is built to keep them out. The only way in runs through giving up the very thing they sell.
 
The other tool a community might already live in is the opposite kind — Discord, or the group chat everyone’s in: two-way by default, free, not trying to administer anyone. The trouble there runs the other way: conversation is all these tools are. The things a community actually has to do — somewhere to put the photos from the event, a vote that counts and stays private, dues that get collected, a directory that stays current, a way to settle who decides what — get improvised on top of a chat box. A pinned message that scrolls away by Tuesday. A Google Form somebody made once. An emoji poll that isn’t binding and isn’t secret. The civic work is real work, and on a general-purpose tool it’s nobody’s job, held together with tape, and it comes apart the moment the person maintaining it drifts off.
 
And the part that does work, the community doesn’t own. Platforms like Discord, Mighty Networks, and Circle will let you run your space and tell you it’s yours — but it’s ownership by permission. They can read it, they set the terms, they can remove you, and what they offer today won’t be what they offer tomorrow — Discord launched ad-free and started running ads in 2024. When a platform like that changes the deal, the result is an exodus, not a quiet downgrade — a whole community scrambling at once to rebuild somewhere else with whatever it can carry out, which is never all of it. Real ownership is the handful of things none of them can offer: the operator can’t read your members’ messages, you can move the whole community to another host with its members and identities intact, no one can delete or sell it out from under you, and no one is mining your members’ attention. Ownership that holds when the platform turns on you — because the alternative isn’t a worry so much as a thing communities live through, on someone else’s schedule.
 
None of which is a reason to switch today, because there’s nothing yet to switch to. This is a few months of design and no running system. The claim is narrower and slower than ‘come use this’ — there is ground here that the people positioned to take it won’t, and that’s where this gets to be built.
 
So the first threat has an answer I’m fairly sure of: the room is real, Front Porch Forum has lived in a version of it for twenty-five years, and the people who could take it would have to become something else to want it. Room to exist is necessary, and nowhere close to sufficient.

The harder threats are inward. The next one is the one a project this size faces from itself: granted there’s room, can a single person actually build something this large — a messaging protocol, a credential system, a federation model, a governance framework — without it collapsing under its own weight long before it’s finished? Which is the question behind a choice that looks, from outside, like exactly the wrong way to go about it: designing the whole thing before building any of it. That’s the next post.
