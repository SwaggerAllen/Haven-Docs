# How does a thing like this survive the person who started it?
 
> *Haven is a federated, end-to-end-encrypted platform built around communities instead of individuals — civic infrastructure, not another social network. This is a journal about designing and building it, in the open.*
 
*Haven — The Slow Play, Part 3*
 
*← The Slow Play, Part 2: [Why design the whole thing before building any of it?](PASTE-POST-5-URL)*
 
In 2006, the founder of Couchsurfing accidentally deleted the site’s database. It was a nonprofit then, run on the promise that it always would be, and when the data was lost the volunteers rebuilt it by hand — unpaid, on their own time, because they believed the promise. Over the years members put in millions of dollars and an enormous amount of free labor on the same understanding. Then, a few years later, after the nonprofit failed to win the tax-exempt status it had been counting on, the founders dissolved it, took venture money, and turned the thing into a company that eventually charged the people who’d built it. Nobody had lied. The founders meant the promise when they made it. The meaning-it just wasn’t holding anything up, because nothing held it up but the meaning-it.
 
That story is close to the default. Mastodon spent nine years as essentially one person’s responsibility before its founder handed it to a nonprofit — nine years in which the whole thing rested on his staying. WordPress is in court because one person ended up holding both the open project and a company competing inside its ecosystem. The pattern repeats because the incentives are real and the protections usually aren’t, and the most common building material for these projects — good intentions — is the one that reliably gives way.
 
Those three are the short version. I worked through eleven of these cases — the ones that held (Signal, Linux, Proton, Mullvad) as well as the ones that didn’t — and the full analysis is published alongside the design docs: [PASTE-FOUNDER-BINDING-URL].
 
“Trust me” was the currency every one of those founders spent, and it bought nothing — not because they were lying, but because a promise nothing enforces is worth nothing however sincerely it’s made. So this post isn’t going to spend it. What I can do instead is show you the machinery meant to make trusting me beside the point.
 
Right now, most of it is still me saying so.
 
## The single point of failure is me
 
The whole project argues that nothing should be the single thing everything rests on — not the individual account, not a central operator, not any one person you’d have to trust. And right now the project is a near-perfect violation of its own thesis. One person holds the design, the direction, the keys to all of it, with nothing between the mission and its betrayal but my own say-so. That is precisely the configuration that failed in every story above. Structurally, right now, nothing separates me from those founders. Not character — they had it too, and meant every word when they gave it. What will separate us is a structure that does not exist yet — a trigger set by people who aren’t me, and a key that actually changes hands — and until that key moves, my word is worth exactly what theirs was worth, which is nothing.
 
One thing has changed, though. Writing all of this down, in public and in this much detail, doesn’t bind me — it wouldn’t have stopped any of the founders I named. What it does is smaller: it gets the project out of my head. Before these documents existed, if I disappeared the work disappeared with me; now the design is out here, reasoned through where it’s settled and marked where it isn’t, and someone could pick it up and run. No one would, yet — writing your own succession plan before you have a successor, or arguably a project, is a bit backwards. But the work is no longer mine alone to lose. That’s the one real step I’ve taken away from the configuration I just admitted to, and it’s a small one.
 
That’s the floor. Everything else is the work of getting off it — building the structure that makes the founder removable rather than essential, so the mission rests on something other than a person who can be bought, or broken, or simply worn down over a decade.
 
## Where the power is meant to go
 
The projects that made it through didn’t survive on better intentions than the ones that didn’t. They survived because they built the structure that shared or surrendered the founder’s power *before* they were forced to — a foundation that owned the company so the company couldn’t be sold out from under the mission, a license that let the community walk away with the work if the stewards went bad, real authority handed to people who weren’t the founder. The lesson isn’t a specific legal form to copy. It’s that the protection has to be built into the structure, ahead of the pressure, because under pressure is exactly when a founder’s good intentions stop being enough.
 
So the question that matters most is the mechanism: how does my authority actually leave my hands — and into whose?
 
I think it should leave them all at once, at a defined moment, not by slow fade. A power that drains away gradually never passes through the one moment legitimacy requires: a point where the people the rules are for get to ratify them — or throw them out and write better ones. Illegitimate power doesn’t become legitimate by fading; it just grows quietly less objectionable, which isn’t the same thing. A single, verifiable handover is the opposite: real in the way a cryptographic key changing hands is real, not a promise to hand it over later. Not scheduled by calendar, because I can’t predict years out when the moment is right, but *triggered* — by the platform reaching a state where there’s a real body to hand authority to.
 
And that moment matters most not for what it does to me but for what it is to everyone else. Up to it, the rules of the whole system are a thing one person wrote and no one agreed to. The handover is where that ends — where the people the rules are for, through representatives they choose and can replace whenever they like, finally have the standing to make the thing their own. The interesting thing about the handoff isn’t a founder stepping back. It’s a constitution that has only ever been a draft, finally meeting the people it’s for.
 
Which is exactly why I will not decide, alone and in advance, what triggers it. The triggering condition determines who gets to be in that room and when, and a founder setting that unilaterally — to govern the same founder — is the snake eating its tail. I also don’t think I *can* set it well yet: I don’t know enough about what the eventual community of communities will look like to choose wisely. So the form of the handover — triggered, verifiable, all at once — is the part I’m fairly sure of. The trigger itself, and who’s in the room when it’s pulled, is not.
 
## The part I wrote alone
 
Here is the problem with everything I just told you. Every word of the governance design — the handover, the structure it hands to, the rules underneath — was written by one person. Me. It has never been put to the people it’s meant to govern, because they aren’t here yet. That’s the thing the convention is for, and it’s also why none of this is settled: a constitution drafted by one person and ratified by no one isn’t a constitution, it’s a proposal. I built it alone because that’s how you get one coherent thing to argue with instead of a committee’s worth of mush, and I stand behind the reasoning. But no one can see their own blind spots, which is the whole reason the draft needs other people before it’s anything more than a draft.
 
So I’ll do here what the last series did with the cryptography: hand over the two places I’m least sure of, at the depth where you can actually engage them. Neither is a puzzle with a clean answer. The first is a paradox in how a thing like this gets founded at all — who legitimately sets the handover in motion, and ratifies it, when the body that does is one I’ve had the heaviest hand in shaping. The second is a real conflict between two things the project won’t give up: a community’s authority over itself, and what’s owed the people inside one when that authority turns on them. Following it leads somewhere I can’t claim to have settled — a question about how much power communities should be able to hold over one another.
 
### Who gets to ratify the founding
 
The handover ratifies the design — but ratified by whom? The body that takes the authority is made of representatives from the member communities, and a community can swap out its representative whenever it likes, so in steady state that body answers to real people. The founding is the hard part. At the moment the first convention is called, that body is small, new, and — this is the bit I can’t design around — substantially assembled by me: I chose which early communities to work with, I set the terms they came in under, and the representatives in the room are there, however indirectly, because of decisions I made. So the convention meant to turn one person’s authority into something legitimate is ratified by a body that one person had the heaviest hand in shaping. It’s the snake eating its tail again, one level up.
 
The design already refuses to let me settle the two things that would decide this alone — when the founding convention triggers, and who exactly is enfranchised to ratify at it. But “decide it with people who aren’t me” is a placeholder, not an answer, because at the founding the people who aren’t me are few and partly of my own choosing. Which people? Enfranchised how? Legitimate by what standard, when the founder is still the gravity the early decisions all bend around? I don’t have a theory of how you hold a legitimate founding for a thing like this — and it’s the first question I’d hand to anyone who’s thought about where constitutions get their authority, because mine doesn’t have its authority yet, and I can’t see how to give it that by myself.
 
### When the exit is held by the people you’d flee
 
The platform’s spine is one-person-one-vote and a refusal to let power concentrate. But it’s also meant to be infrastructure for real institutions, and real institutions mostly aren’t democracies. The nonprofit that is, in practice, its founding director. The neighborhood association two families have run for twenty years. The congregation whose pastor has the final word. A platform that only fit communities governed by vote would be useless to most of the communities it’s for — and worse, it would be doing the exact thing it refuses to do at the top: imposing one model of the good on everyone, deciding from the outside how other people have to run their own common life. So it has to support the benevolent dictator and the founder who never really hands off and every other arrangement that doesn’t run on a head count — which means deliberately building the power to be undemocratic inside a community into a system whose whole premise is that power shouldn’t concentrate.
 
That much I think I can hold: communities are sovereign over themselves; the platform offers mechanisms, not mandates; one-person-one-vote governs the platform, not the community. What I can’t yet answer is what happens when that internal authority goes bad — a tenants’ union whose organizer stops calling votes, a mutual-aid network whose founders quietly close the circle. For a single person, the answer holds up: you can always leave. Your identity was never the community’s to keep — you walk out with your keys, your history, yourself, and go find or start another community. A captured leadership can expel you or silence you, but it can’t trap you, because you were never locked in; the door was never theirs to hold.
 
Where it breaks is leaving *together*. If a faction wants to split off and take its shared life with it — the history, the continuity, the thing it spent years building as a group — that’s a schism, and a schism runs on a threshold: how many have to agree before the group can cleave and carry its identity out. Set it low and any disgruntled handful can fracture a healthy community. Set it high and a captured leadership that controls it can make collective exit impossible: each person can still walk out alone, stripped of everything the group built together, but the group itself can’t leave whole. Individual exit stays open; the captor just makes sure no one can take the community with them. The check I lean on hardest holds for the person and fails for the group, in the one case it most needs to work — the captured community, where the one who’d block the schism is the one you’d flee.
 
I don’t have a clean answer, and I’ve come to think a clean one would be the mistake. The platform could just cap the threshold — forbid any community from setting it past some line — but that’s the platform reaching down to dictate how communities run themselves, the exact move it refuses everywhere else. The only direction that doesn’t put me in that chair is sideways. Communities aren’t islands; they ally, federate, lend each other standing and reach. An alliance could require, as a condition of belonging, that its members keep a schism threshold the leadership can’t quietly rig — so a community that locks its people in forfeits its place in the wider network. Pressure between peers, instead of policy from above.
 
But that only works if an alliance can hold its members to anything at all, and the moment it can, you’ve raised the oldest question in federation: how much may the union compel the member before the union is the thing to fear? That isn’t a cryptographic problem — it’s state against federal power, the line every federation has bled over, in civic-software clothes. I haven’t solved it. What the design does is force the question to be answered by the communities, through their alliances, rather than by me from the top — which is the most I’ll claim. How strong an alliance should be, and what it may demand of its members to protect the people inside them, is the second thing I’d hand to someone who studies how power moves between institutions, not just within them.
 
## The half of this that no structure fixes
 
Everything so far is about surviving *capture*. There’s a plainer survival the structure can’t touch: the work right now is more than one person should be doing, and no governance fixes that. It isn’t a design problem — you can’t architect your way out of being one person. On a project meant to run a decade, the unsentimental math is that a capable person added early is worth more than a year of me working alone. Not because three people do the work of three — they don’t, and piling bodies onto a late project is the oldest mistake in software — but because what a team builds compounds, and what I build alone is one person’s year. Early, the highest-leverage thing I can do is stop being the only one.
 
It isn’t one missing person, either — not a single cofounder who rides in and solves everything. It’s more than one kind of work.
 
I can do most of it as a capable generalist — well enough to carry it for a while, not well enough to do it excellently while also writing the protocol. It’s the project’s own thesis, turned on me: a single point that does everything competently and nothing exceptionally is still a single point. And the part I least can do from a desk isn’t the cryptography. Getting a real organization onto this is the slow work of being handed a community’s trust by someone who already holds it — the same in-person way the platform asks its own members to be vouched for — and of learning what an institution needs before building for it, on a human clock no software shortens. That work is as hard as the cryptography, and just as much rests on it. Each person who joins with real expertise in it makes the project faster, better, and further-reaching than one person could manage alone.
 
## Why this, and not something easier
 
The reason any of this is worth a decade isn’t really about where people gather. It’s about who holds the power in the places they gather, and how little of it they can see. The platforms that swallowed the commons don’t just host the conversation — they own it: they decide what’s surfaced and what’s buried, who may speak and who’s quietly removed, accountable to no one it happens to. And under that visible power is a quieter one. The same systems measure us, sell what they learn, and tune what we’re shown — inside a machine it’s hard to step out of, because everyone you’d step out to reach is in it too. Haven is an attempt at a kind of place that refuses all of that — though not all of it the same way. The operator who can read you, the machine that measures and tunes you: those it refuses by construction, now, today. The power that decides who may speak and answers to no one it happens to: that it refuses by a structure I’m still building — the one I don’t yet have. The first is already true. The second is the bet the whole project is.
