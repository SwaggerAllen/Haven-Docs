# Where this stands, and what I’m stuck on
 
*Introducing Haven, Part 3*
 
*← Part 2: [We built the internet as if every man were an island](PASTE-POST-2-URL)*
 
A word on what this is. It’s me, one person, designing Haven in the open — no team yet, no funding, no product you can use. What there is: a fairly large body of design work, most of which I think is right, and a handful of genuinely hard problems I haven’t solved. This post is the inventory of both, and the map for where the journal goes from here.
 
I’m doing this in the open on purpose. The whole project rests on a claim that you can build infrastructure people can trust, and you don’t get to make that claim from behind a curtain. You get the reasoning, the decisions, and the parts I’m unsure of, in roughly the order I work them.
 
One more thing belongs in the open: these documents were drafted with substantial AI assistance. One person could not have organized a system this size this quickly without it, and pretending otherwise would be a strange way to start a project about trust. The architecture, every decision in it, and every mistake in it are mine.
 
So here’s the shape I’m confident in. The entity model from the last post — people inside communities, communities hosting on instances, instances federating, communities banding into alliances — is settled; that’s the skeleton, and it holds. The split between the operator who runs a server and the governance that decides what the server may do is settled, and a lot rests on it: it’s what lets a community fire its host without losing itself. Schism — a community splitting cleanly in two, both halves keeping their history — is settled in shape, though it touches one of the open problems below and may shift at the edges. And the credential ecosystem is settled in its everyday form: communities and outside issuers vouch for facts about a person, and the holder can prove just the fact a given community asks for and nothing more — each community sets its own bar for who gets in. That last piece is the part of the identity problem I’m *not* stuck on, and saying so here is what lets the hard version of it, problem 5 below, be narrow.
 
What’s *not* settled is a small number of specific technical problems. Two of them are genuinely open — I know the shape of the answer but not the answer, and they’re where I most need a cryptographer. Three more are specified down to a narrow residual: the construction is chosen and what’s left is small enough to name. And there’s one result I think might be true that would be very good news if it is; I’ve put it inside the problem it bears on, as the bet it currently is rather than the fact I’d like it to be. As the last post said: the architecture is simple in shape; the cryptography that has to hold it up is not.
 
I’ll write about each in more depth down the line; this is enough to tell whether it’s the kind of thing you’d want to help with.
 
**1. Verifiable abuse reporting without building a surveillance archive (message franking).** In an end-to-end encrypted system, a moderator needs to confirm that reported content was really sent by the accused — otherwise reports can be fabricated — but the mechanism that makes a report verifiable must not turn every past message into permanent attributable evidence a future hostile party could mine. Message franking is the literature that addresses this. The shape of the answer is a commitment the sender produces at send time and an opening the recipient can later hand to a moderator: with the opening, a report verifies; without it, the commitment proves nothing, so deniability survives everywhere outside an actual report. What I don’t have settled is the composition. Franking was worked out for two-party messaging; Haven needs it to sit on top of MLS group messaging *and* on the sender-key broadcast layer that carries alliance-wide content, and the encryption underneath has to be the committing kind — an AEAD where one ciphertext can’t be opened two different ways. Get that wrong and you’ve built what the literature nicknamed an invisible salamander — the failure where a single ciphertext decrypts to two valid plaintexts and the whole verifiability claim quietly collapses. I know that’s the hazard and I know the primitive that’s supposed to avoid it; what I need is someone who can check the composition actually delivers it across both layers, rather than each layer alone.
 
**2. Composing MLS with a sender-key broadcast layer.** Haven’s alliance messaging runs on two layers because one layer can’t do both jobs. The governance layer — the representatives who actually decide things — is a standard MLS group, small enough to get MLS’s full guarantees: forward secrecy, recovery after a key compromise, deniability. The broadcast layer, which carries alliance-wide content out to everyone in every member community, is a sender-key scheme, because MLS doesn’t scale to a whole alliance’s worth of readers. The reason is structural: MLS gives every member their own evolving key state and rederives shared secrets as the membership changes, which is what buys the strong guarantees — but the cost of that machinery grows with the group, and an alliance-wide readership is the wrong size for it. A sender-key scheme drops to a weaker, cheaper arrangement: each sender has one key the readers share, good enough for broadcast that’s authenticated and confidential at the boundary but not trying to protect members from each other. The claim I need to hold is that the broad key distribution on the broadcast layer doesn’t quietly weaken the confidentiality of the governance layer underneath it — that the two share a community’s cryptographic state without leaking into each other, and that I can say precisely what’s protected at the boundary between them and what isn’t. I’m not claiming secrecy from alliance members on the broadcast layer; when thousands of people can read something, “secret from members” isn’t a property any protocol delivers, and pretending otherwise is how you get a design that lies. I’m claiming confidentiality at the alliance’s outer boundary and honest containment of what a compromise on one layer costs the other. This is the one I most want a real cryptographer to attack, because unlike the others it isn’t a charted problem with a known answer I’m implementing — it’s a composition of two well-understood pieces into something the literature hasn’t blessed, and novel composition is exactly where confident-looking designs break.
 
Those two are the open frontier. The next three are a different kind of unsure — the construction is chosen, and what’s left is narrow enough that I can name it.
 
**3. Equivocation defense.** A federated instance must not be able to tell different stories to different peers — show one version of events here and a contradicting one there — without the contradiction being catchable. Several trust properties downstream of federation fall apart if it can. This is a solved problem in shape: it’s what Certificate Transparency does for the web’s certificate authorities, and Haven uses the same machinery — an append-only log of what each instance has committed to, with independent witnesses co-signing the log’s heads so no instance can maintain two divergent versions without the split becoming visible. The specific decision I’ve made is to ride the shared transparency-log ecosystem rather than build a private one — the witness-coordination work happening in that community is exactly what Haven needs, and there are running reference implementations to learn from. So this one isn’t conceptually open; the residual is engineering and governance. The engineering: the mature implementations are built for the web-certificate world, and porting that to Haven’s content is real work I haven’t done. The governance: a witness network is only as good as the independence of its witnesses, and who they are is a question about institutions, not cryptography. I’m fairly confident here. I’m naming it because “fairly confident” is not “done,” and the gap between them is where I’d want a second set of eyes.
 
**4. Cross-mailbox causality under adversarial conditions.** A mailbox is Haven’s basic unit of stored state — roughly, one feed or one relationship’s worth of messages, owned by a single instance. Within one mailbox there’s a single authoritative writer and a clean canonical order, so no agreement protocol is needed; that’s by design. The hard part is operations that span mailboxes — schism, migration, alliance admission, operator transition — where the parties don’t trust each other and order itself can be lied about, withheld, or replayed. What makes me reasonably calm about this one is that the architecture sidesteps the worst version of the problem rather than trying to win it. There’s no shared global state that multiple instances have to agree on; every meaningful resource has exactly one authoritative owner, and cross-mailbox operations are framed as references to specific committed content rather than as facts about everyone’s current state. That choice means Haven never takes on the hardest class of federation problem — the Byzantine, DAG-shaped state-agreement problems where mutually distrustful servers have to converge on a shared history and an attacker games the convergence. Matrix lives in that class by design, and in 2025 it cost them: a pair of disclosed protocol vulnerabilities where weaknesses in cross-server state resolution let a participating server corrupt a room’s shared state. That’s the harder problem, and Matrix took it on for a reason — convergent shared state lets you do things single authority can’t. Choosing single authority isn’t a free win, then: it means Haven simply can’t offer that kind of multi-writer shared state, and I’m betting that what Haven does doesn’t need it. What it buys in return is that there’s no convergence for an attacker to game. If Haven ever ships, it’ll be partly because it held to that bet wherever it could, and this is the clearest case. The residual: I’ve written down the specific guarantees these cross-mailbox operations make and the three places they don’t — they lean on loosely-synced clocks within bounds, they can’t catch multiple hostile operators colluding to tell one consistent lie, and they promise eventual consistency, not a synchronized global “now.” The open work is turning the guarantees into precise protocol and confirming none of them smuggles in a cross-instance agreement requirement through the back door — which would undo the very thing that keeps this simple.
 
**5. One human, one identity — under a hostile issuer.** The everyday half of this lives in the snapshot above: communities and outside issuers vouch for facts, and each community sets its own bar. The open half is narrower and sharper, and it’s the least-pinned of the five. The anti-abuse story from the last post — that infiltrating a community costs real, vouched-for humans rather than cheap server cycles — rests on a one-human-to-one-credential binding that’s non-transferable, non-poolable, and holds even when the issuer goes bad. I’m building it on BBS, and the construction stacks a few properties on a single primitive. At its core is one blind-issued uniqueness credential carrying a committed secret. From that secret, per-verifier pseudonyms give within-community recognizability and cross-community unlinkability — you’re recognizable inside a community and unlinkable across them. And per-context nullifiers give the cardinality limits: one vote, N per event. All of it derives from the one credential. (BBS, blind issuance, per-verifier linkability — the relevant CFRG drafts, work in progress, which I weight accordingly.) The two questions I’d hand a cryptographer first: whether blind issuance, selective disclosure, per-verifier pseudonyms, and nullifier soundness actually hold *simultaneously* over a single blind-committed identifier, or whether some pair degrades another; and the integration surface for the shared-secret equality proof that links two credentials to the same hidden holder — a standard technique whose soundness lives in the composition detail. Two costs come with it. First, universal uniqueness needs a real-world dedup anchor at issuance — an external proof check — and that one check is the place the privacy isn’t free (minimized to a one-way hash, kept out of every ordinary presentation, but not eliminable). Second, BBS is pairing-based, not post-quantum, with no mature PQ replacement for anonymous credentials yet, so the layer sits behind a replaceable interface that is a promise to my future self, not a solved migration.
 
And here’s the part I find genuinely exciting, offered as a bet rather than a result. With blind issuance done right, a *compromised* issuer — a corrupted community, a captured instance — still can’t forge an existing member’s identity, because it never learned the secret that identity derives from. The worst it can do is mint fresh fakes: sybils, which are detectable and bounded and exactly what the rest of the system is built to absorb. If that holds, it inverts the usual failure mode — in most systems a compromised authority can impersonate *anyone*, which is catastrophic; here it could only manufacture *new* nobodies, which is a nuisance. That would be a real result, and a reason the architecture is shaped the way it is. I want to stress it’s *if*: it depends on the precise blind-issuance property of the construction, and confirming it is one of the things I most want a cryptographer to either nail down or knock down. I’d rather show you the thing I’m hoping is true and let you break it than quietly assume it and build on sand. The same primitive shows up in voting and ticketing too, which is part of why getting it right matters past identity alone.
 
One caution sits above all five. For each problem I’ve found a construction in the literature that looks like it fits — but that’s matching, not analysis, and analysis is the part I don’t have the expertise to do. The risk I’d worry about most isn’t inside any single problem; it’s an adversary working several at once — equivocating across mailboxes while a franking gap opens during a schism, say. Whether these pieces hold up on their own, and whether they hold up in combination, is exactly the kind of thing I need a cryptographer for, and the part I’d least trust my own eyes on.
 
There’s a whole other layer of hard questions further out — how governance holds up at scale, how I eventually hand off the parts of this that shouldn’t stay in one person’s hands — and I know they’re there; they’re just not the near-term work, and I’d rather solve what’s in front of me honestly than wave at everything at once.
 
**A few practical notes.** There’s no code yet — the work right now is getting the design to where it’s worth an expert’s time to review, and worth a contributor’s time to build on. When there’s code, you’ll see it here first. As for this journal: roughly weekly to monthly, each post taking one idea or one problem, opening with the human version of why it matters, going as deep into the technical core as the problem needs, and ending with what falls out of it. Some posts will be me solving something. Some will be me stuck, in public, hoping someone reads it and says “you’ve got that wrong.” Both are the point.
 
**Who I’m hoping to hear from.** Cryptographers who want to bang on the problems above — especially 1 and 2 — and tell me where they break. People who can read a composition claim and find the gap I can’t see. And, more loosely, people who care about the civic problem underneath all this and want to follow along while it turns into something real. I’m not asking anyone to commit to anything. I’m asking the right people to find me.
 
The thing I most believe about this work is that the parts that feel airtight are the ones to be most suspicious of. I’ve pointed at the parts that don’t, as best I can see them. If I’ve gotten something wrong — and somewhere in the problems above I almost certainly have — I’d rather find out from you now than from a flaw later.
 
I’d be glad of the company. Come tell me where I’m wrong.
