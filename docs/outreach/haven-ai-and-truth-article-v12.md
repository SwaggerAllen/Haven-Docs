# The AI Essay

*AI is making it harder to trust anything on a screen — at the worst possible moment.*

*Everybody’s writing about AI right now, so here’s mine — on what it’s going to do to trust.*

> *Haven is a federated, end-to-end-encrypted platform built around communities instead of individuals — civic infrastructure, not another social network. This is a journal about designing and building it, in the open.*

---

Every word of Haven’s design was written by a machine.

So were these posts. So will the code be, when there’s code.

The decisions are mine, and so are the mistakes. The words came through a model.

And the same technology that wrote all of this is one of the reasons I can’t stop building it.

---

For most of my life, some things counted as proof.

A photograph counted. A recording counted. A voice on the phone was your mother’s voice, and that was the end of the argument. Video was what you showed when no one would believe you. None of it was perfect — fakes existed — but faking it well was expensive and rare, so seeing was, most of the time, a reason to believe.

That’s over.

Not ending. Over.

A machine can make a photograph of a thing that never happened. It can make your mother’s voice say words she never said, out of a few seconds of her talking. It can make the video. It’s improving on a curve that doesn’t look like it bends back, and the results are already past the point where your eye can catch them.

Everyone’s first worry is scams, and the scams are coming. But that’s the shallow fear.

The deep one is this: evidence is ceasing to be a category.

When any image can be made, no image proves anything. When any voice can be cloned, a voice proves nothing.

You won’t mainly be fooled by fakes. You’ll be right to disbelieve what’s real. The forgery doesn’t just deceive you — it strips the genuine thing next to it of any power to convince.

Something is being taken from us that we never thought to guard, because it never needed guarding: the plain ability to be believed.

---

This is not landing on a healthy society.

We spent decades letting the rooms empty out. The union hall. The congregation. The bowling league. The lodge. The block that knew its own kids. The porch you actually sat on. The places where you became *known* — slowly, in person, by people who’d still be there next year — thinned and closed and got swapped for feeds, and we mostly let it happen, because the feed was easier and right there and free.

And it turned out the feed couldn’t carry the weight.

You can have five thousand connections and not one person who’d notice you were gone.

This isn’t a mood I’m projecting onto the country. It’s measured. Even before the pandemic, something like half of American adults were reporting real loneliness, and in 2023 the Surgeon General named it an epidemic. COVID didn’t start it — it broke habits and continuity that were already thinning, the standing Tuesday and the room you could walk into without an invitation, and a lot of those never came back. The young are hit hardest: the people who grew up the most connected report the highest rates of all, and the last few years have not turned it around.

So we’ve built, without ever once deciding to, a world where a person can be constantly, totally, technologically connected and still have no one. No room that’s theirs.

That should frighten us more than any deepfake, because a forgery only matters if there’s still something real left for it to crowd out.

And the timing is almost too cruel to be accidental: the perfect counterfeit of human contact arrives in force at the precise moment the real thing is at its lowest ebb in living memory. We hollowed out the commons, and then we built a machine that sells us a hologram of it and calls the hologram connection.

That is a fucking tragedy.

---

So if proof can no longer live in the signal — because every signal can now be faked — it has to come off the wire entirely.

There is one place left to stand, and it’s old.

A person. In a room.

Known to other people over time, with something of their own to lose if they vouch for a liar. Not a verified badge. Not a blue check. Not a cryptographic attestation, which rides the same network that can carry a forged one just as easily. A body, present, recognized by other bodies that were also present.

That kind of proof is analog — the one thing the machine can’t generate, because it isn’t information. It’s a human being spending their own standing, in person, on you.

You can fake the photo of the handshake. You cannot fake the handshake.

I won’t dress it up as wisdom. It isn’t a deep truth about the soul. It’s just the last ground that isn’t underwater.

And under all the architecture, the thing I believe most is simpler than any of it: people are hungry for this — to be known, and let in by someone who means it — and they keep being handed thinner and thinner substitutes and told to be grateful.

---

Which is the thing Haven is built around, and I keep turning over how strangely it fits this moment.

In Haven you don’t join a community by filling out a form. You’re brought in, in person, by someone already inside, who stakes their own standing on you. That’s the front door, and most of the defense against bad actors follows from it. To flood a community with fakes you don’t beat an algorithm; you need real humans willing to physically show up and vouch, and to burn their own reputation when the people they vouched for turn out to be fakes. The cost is paid in people and relationships, not server cycles — the one cost a machine can’t drive to zero, because it was never a technical quantity.

I didn’t design that to fight AI.

I designed it because it’s how human communities have always sorted real from fake — you don’t earn trust by registering, you earn it by showing up and being known by people who stake something on you. I was copying something old because it was the only thing I’d ever seen actually work.

It turns out the old thing is also the one defense that doesn’t rot. Every digital wall against forgery gets cheaper to climb each year. The in-person handoff gets no cheaper — it’s priced in human time and human reputation, and those don’t fall when the next model ships.

It doesn’t un-fake the world, to be clear. A vouched door can’t tell you whether the video on your feed is real, or whether the voice on the phone is your mother — only who’s actually in the room with you. That’s a smaller promise, and it’s the one I can keep.

It has a cost. An in-person, vouched door is about the steepest on-ramp in consumer software. Haven grows at the speed of people actually meeting each other, which is slow — maybe the slowest way anyone has chosen to grow a thing like this.

I’m paying it on purpose. It’s the only port in the storm.

And the bet underneath all of it is just that hunger: that enough people want this badly enough to pay full price for it.

---

I’m trying to build a place where the real thing is possible again. Not because I’m certain it works — there’s plenty I haven’t solved. Because I think a great many people lost something they never knew could be taken, they can feel the hole where it used to be, and somebody should at least try to build a door back to it before the fakes paper over the fact that it’s gone.

That’s the part that gets me out of bed.

— Allen
