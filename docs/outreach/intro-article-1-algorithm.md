# What if the problem isn’t the algorithm?
 
*Introducing Haven, Part 1*

I have a friend who tends bar. He hates Facebook — the noise, the ads, the way it makes him feel when he’s done scrolling. He’s tried to leave a few times. He can’t. Not because he’s hooked, but because Facebook is where his regulars find out which bar he’s working and which nights. Leaving wouldn’t free him; it would cost him his customers. So he stays somewhere he doesn’t want to be, because the thing he actually needs — a way for his people to find him — got captured by a platform he’d otherwise walk away from.
 
I think about him a lot, because I’m in a milder version of the same trap. I got off social media years ago, around the time it stopped being good for me. And I still have a Facebook account, because more than once the apartment building I lived in ran its announcements through a Messenger group, and the choice was rejoin or miss the notice that the water was getting shut off. I left. It didn’t matter. The platform had already eaten the thing I’d have used instead.
 
That’s the part most critiques of social media miss, I think. The usual story is that these platforms are *bad for you* and you should *log off* — as if logging off were available. For a lot of people it isn’t, because the platform isn’t just where you waste time. It’s where your kid’s school posts the schedule, where your customers find you, where your building tells you the water’s off. It ate the commons, and now opting out means opting out of things that aren’t optional.
 
So when people ask what’s wrong with these platforms, I don’t think “log off” is an answer — and I don’t think the popular answer is quite right either.
 
## The usual diagnosis
 
Ask most people what’s broken about these platforms and you’ll hear some version of: the algorithm. The feed is tuned to enrage you because enragement is engagement; the ranking optimizes for time-on-site; the machine learned that outrage and anxiety keep you scrolling, so it serves you outrage and anxiety. That’s all true, as far as it goes. The attention economy is real and it’s ugly, and a lot of good people are working on de-ranking the feed, or making the feed chronological, or letting you choose your own algorithm.
 
I think that’s a real problem. I just don’t think it’s the *deepest* one. Because every one of those fixes takes the same thing for granted: that the basic unit of the system is *you* — an individual account, broadcasting to a following, and the only question is how the firehose of everyone’s broadcasts gets sorted. Change the ranking and you still have the same machine: individuals, shouting outward, into a feed. You’ve adjusted the sorting. You haven’t touched the shape.
 
The deeper problem, I think, is about trust and scale. The kind of trust that actually holds a group together — where people more or less know each other, and someone who harms the group has to keep living among the people they harmed — works at the scale of a few hundred people. Past that it thins out, and you need something else to stand in for it. Online, we mostly didn’t build that something; we just kept piling more people into the same feed. So the scammer can burn one person or ten thousand and simply vanish, his life untouched by the damage he did — there’s nothing he has to keep living inside. Sorting the feed differently does nothing about that. It’s not a ranking problem; it’s the social fabric the ranking sits on top of, and you can’t patch that by re-ordering a feed.
 
## The cut I’m making
 
Here’s the claim, as plainly as I can put it: the problem isn’t how the feed is sorted. The problem is that the *individual* is the unit and the *community* is an afterthought.
 
Think about where your actual life happens. Not online — in the room. It happens in communities: your congregation, your kid’s school, the block, the volunteer crew, the friends who’ve known you twenty years. The school I mentioned a minute ago, whose schedule lives in a Facebook Group — that’s not a Facebook Group. That’s a community a couple hundred families actually belong to, and the software can only see it as a page with some posts on it. These groups have rules, histories, ways of letting people in and easing people out. They’re real social objects. And on every major platform, the software can barely see them for what they are. The thing that matters most in your social life is the thing the software models worst.
 
Seen that way, “fix the algorithm” stops being the answer, because the algorithm was never the thing in the wrong place. The *unit* was. And if you move the unit — if you build a system where the community, not the account, is the first-class thing, the durable thing, the thing everything is organized around — then some of the familiar problems don’t get *solved* so much as they stop being expressible. The clearest case: a system built around communities doesn’t have a global outrage feed to fix, because it doesn’t have a global feed at all — nothing was removed, there was simply never a place to put one.
 
## What this journal is
 
A word on what this is. This is a journal about building a thing called Haven, written as I go — what I’ve figured out, what I’ve decided, and the parts I’m still stuck on. There’s no product to sign up for and nothing to buy. I’m not going to try to win you over in one post. The argument is cumulative — it depends on how the pieces fit — so I’d rather build it piece by piece than flatten it into a pitch.
 
The next piece is the obvious question: what would a system look like if the community, not the person, were the basic unit?
 
That’s where I’ll go next.
 
— Allen
