# Start here

Haven is civic infrastructure built around communities instead of individual accounts — federated, end-to-end encrypted, and governed by the people inside it rather than the company hosting it. This is the short version: the core idea, what follows from it, and how the parts connect. The longer arguments live in the essays and the full design in the [architecture document](docs/design/civic-platform-architecture.md); I link to both as the threads come up.

## The wrong unit

Most accounts of what’s broken about online platforms point at the algorithm — the ranking tuned to enrage you, the feed optimized for time-on-site. That’s real, but it’s downstream. The [deeper problem is the unit](docs/outreach/intro-article-1-algorithm.md).

On every mainstream platform the first-class thing is the individual account. You are a profile; you broadcast; the software is built around a person speaking to an audience. The communities where your actual life happens — the school, the block, the congregation, the union local, the co-op — are an afterthought the software can barely see, approximated by a page with posts on it. The structure that makes them real (who belongs, who decides, what the rules are, what happened before you arrived, what happens when people fundamentally disagree) has nowhere to live. And because so much civic life has moved onto these platforms, opting out has stopped being available: you can’t leave the place your kid’s school posts the schedule.

Haven moves the unit. Communities are the primary entity, with that structure modeled directly rather than approximated: membership, roles, rules, history, governance, and even the ability to split. Individual broadcast is not a first-class operation; people speak through their institutional contexts, the way they mostly do offline. There is no global feed — not removed, just never built, because with the community as the unit there’s no global “everyone” to aggregate. The app still lives in your pocket like any other; the life it serves doesn’t, and isn’t supposed to. [A second essay](docs/outreach/intro-article-2-island.md) works through what falls out once you take that inversion seriously.

Everything below follows from that one move. The choices can look arbitrary one at a time; they cohere because they come from a single observation — civic life is already structured, and a platform that models that structure inherits it rather than inventing a worse one.

## What moving the unit changes

**Trust is inherited, not invented.** Communities already establish trust — in-person introduction, vouching, credentialing, reputation, governance, the willingness to walk away. Haven builds on those mechanisms instead of substituting software for them. The most visible consequence is how you join: membership takes an in-person handshake — a QR or Bluetooth exchange with an existing member — not a click. That’s the gate that makes infiltration cost real, vouched-for humans rather than cheap server cycles, and it’s the whole anti-abuse story rather than a separate system bolted on top. It has a real cost, named plainly: it’s about the steepest on-ramp in consumer software, and the platform grows at the speed of people actually meeting. That cost is smallest exactly where I mean to start — communities that already gather in person.

**Privacy is structural, not a setting.** Everything inside a community is end-to-end encrypted; the operator hosting it can’t read its contents or even tell what kind of community it is. No operator, advertiser, or platform company is positioned to read your members’ messages, because the architecture withholds the keys, not because a policy asks nicely. This is also the ground [incumbents can’t follow onto](docs/outreach/intro-article-4-timescale.md): a vendor whose product is the readable database can’t go operator-blind without dismantling the thing it sells. Verifiable abuse reporting is designed to survive that — a recipient can prove a message was really sent without any third party reading the channel, so “no one can read this” doesn’t mean “no recourse.” Making that property hold cryptographically is one of the open problems below.

**Communities govern themselves — and the platform makes no policy.** Once the operator can’t see in, governance and moderation have nowhere to live but inside the community. So Haven provides primitives (members, roles, votes, proposals, the ability to split) that communities configure into whatever they already use: majority, supermajority, consensus, sortition, representative structures, voting windows that match a synod or an annual meeting. When a decision splits a community badly, **schism** is a first-class operation: the dissenting faction forks into a new community carrying its history, credentials, alliances, and reputation, rather than decamping to other software and severing everyone on both sides. Exit is built in.

**Alliances are social; federation is plumbing.** This distinction is load-bearing. An **alliance** is communities choosing to cooperate — sharing moderation, recognizing each other’s members, keeping a common calendar, even lending jurors to each other’s moderation cases when a dispute needs outside neutrality. **Federation** is just servers interoperating, so a member on one can participate in a community on another. Alliance is affiliation; federation is infrastructure; the two are never the same. An instance, in turn, is an operator (root, the hosting bill, legal responsibility) plus a governance layer that holds the instance’s durable identity — which is why a community is never trapped on a machine: fire the host, and it reconstitutes elsewhere carrying its identity with it. Keeping the pilot honest to those seams, so that federation is later an expansion and not a rewrite, is what [federation readiness](docs/design/civic-platform-federation-readiness.md) commits the design to.

**Identity splits the way trust does.** Two kinds of credential, two kinds of issuer. *Humanness* — am I a distinct real person — is universal, used for cross-community sybil resistance, and issued from external proof. *Decency* — am I a member here, in good standing, holding this role — is local, issued by communities themselves, because a church verifying its own members is just part of being a church. Humanness is universal; decency is local. The design is honest that humanness ultimately rests on an offline human act and can’t be manufactured by cryptography — a floor it states plainly rather than papers over.

**The money can’t quietly turn extractive.** Revenue comes mainly from producing physical credentials — member cards and the like — which scales with institutional adoption, not captured attention. No advertising, no engagement optimization, no data sale, no cut on community commerce. The exclusion is structural: feeds are typed and chronological, with no amplification engine to sell and no surveillance signal to target. A local business can buy a spot on the bulletin board; no one can buy amplification, because that machinery was never built.

## How the platform governs itself

A platform that claims to resist its own operator has to say how. The answer is **math-enabled democracy**: cryptographic mechanisms enforce the procedural rules — who’s authorized, whether a threshold of signatures is met, what was decided — while humans make the judgments those procedures enable. Pure social governance can be overridden by whoever holds the keys; pure mechanical governance can’t handle the cases the rules didn’t anticipate. Governance weight follows the *people-vote* principle: it derives from individual humans, split fractionally across the communities someone belongs to, so it tracks people rather than accounts, tokens, or capital. The [whole-system governance](docs/design/haven-whole-system-governance.md) document develops this, along with the layered authority that runs from the community up to the platform.

Underneath sits a deliberate [two-entity structure](docs/design/haven-organizational-structure.md). **Strutco**, a California public benefit corporation, builds and operates the platform — and is structurally just one operator among future many, not the platform itself. The **Haven Foundation**, an independent 501(c)(3), is the trust anchor: it holds the trademark, the protocol, the certification processes, and platform-level governance authority. Its board is the same body as the governance representatives, so legal and cryptographic authority are exercised by the same people; neither can act without the other.

## The pattern underneath

The throughline across all of this is anti-capture by making exit always possible and never free. A community can fork through schism. The trust anchor can be forked too — the governing alliance has no privileged path and can be competed with, so its authority comes from stewardship and accumulated recognition, not protocol privilege. And the founder is meant to be bound by an explicit succession structure rather than trusted to stay good — the [problem of surviving the founder](docs/outreach/intro-article-6-survival.md), which the design is most candid about, because the structure doesn’t fully exist yet. “Trust me” is the currency that bought nothing in every comparable project that meant to stay aligned and didn’t; the work is to make trusting the founder beside the point.

For a community, it comes down to four guarantees that hold even against the operator:

- The operator cannot read your members’ messages.
- You can move the whole community to another host with members and identities intact.
- No one can delete or sell the community out from under you.
- No one is mining your members’ attention.

Taken one at a time, some of these exist elsewhere — end-to-end encryption most obviously, which Signal, WhatsApp, and others already offer. What’s rare is the whole set at once, held structurally rather than granted. “Ownership” on other platforms is permission — branding, data export, admin rights, all conferred by an operator who can revoke them. These four hold whether or not the operator consents, which is what makes them different in kind.

## Where the project is

Haven is in its design phase — [worked out in full before it’s built](docs/outreach/intro-article-5-methodology.md), heading toward a small pilot with a single founding community. That sequence is deliberate: the layers constrain each other hard, so a wrong assumption low in the stack would force a rebuild cascade through everything above it. The build itself comes in [six phases](docs/design/civic-platform-features.md), each one a platform a real community could use, from the cut-down pilot to a mature system with a plugin layer. The pilot is the least-specified part of the whole design, on purpose: its details wait on the finished design and on the actual first community, neither of which should be guessed ahead of.

Substantial pieces are settled — the entity model, the governance framework, the organizational structure, moderation, operator transitions. Substantial pieces are open, and named as such: the cryptographic constructions await external review and shouldn’t be read as verified. The sharpest of them — verifiable abuse reporting, composing two messaging layers without weakening either, one-human-one-identity under a hostile issuer — are laid out as [the problems I’m stuck on](docs/outreach/intro-article-3-stuck.md), an open invitation to anyone who can break them. Almost all of this publishes as it matures, unsettled directions and unflattering findings included, because designing in the open is part of how the founder is made replaceable.
